\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In an encrypted mempool~\cite{PP26}, block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run or sandwich a transaction based on its contents. The central question is who opens the transactions afterwards. Current designs give three answers, each at a cost.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A committee} holds a threshold decryption key and opens what was ordered, as in Shutter~\cite{Shutter}. Privacy then rests on no threshold of its members colluding, and liveness on enough of them being online.
  \item \textbf{A trusted execution environment} opens transactions inside hardware whose vendor and side channels must be trusted.
  \item \textbf{The sender} opens its own transaction after the ordering, as in sealed transactions and the self-decryption path of LUCID~\cite{LUCID}. No other party is trusted, but the sender sees the ordering first and can withhold its key when the ordering is unfavorable, which gives it a free option.
\end{itemize}
Hybrid designs~\cite{WM25} let either the sender or a committee open, which removes the free option while the committee is online and the committee's liveness problem while the sender is honest. Their failure case remains a dishonest sender while the committee is offline.

We present \leanseal, a fallback for self-decryption that needs no committee: no designated party has to be online or trusted for a withheld transaction to be disclosed. The sender opens its own transaction in the normal case, but it attaches a time-lock puzzle that anyone can solve to recover the key after a fixed amount of sequential computation, about 30 seconds on Ethereum. If the puzzle is well formed and the transaction is encrypted under its key, a sender that withholds its key can still delay or cancel the transaction's execution, but cannot keep the transaction hidden. The reveal and the puzzle together form a timed commitment~\cite{BN00}. The puzzle uses only a hash function such as SHA-256: it needs no trusted setup and no hardware trust, and its idealized version is secure against quantum openers.

The puzzle is the chained-hash puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan~\cite{MMV11}, in which the sender computes as many hashes as an opener, but in parallel (\cref{fig:schedules}). They also proved that no puzzle built only from hashes can make the sender's work grow more slowly than the delay, so the cost of \leanseal falls on the sender. This paper examines whether that cost is acceptable for sealing transactions (\cref{tab:glance}), and what an encrypted mempool built on the puzzle can and cannot provide.

\subsection{Our results}\label{sec:results}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A design.} We describe how \leanseal serves as the decryption path of an encrypted mempool (\cref{sec:mempool}). It uses the same data as LUCID and the hybrid design, a ciphertext bound to a commitment to its key, so its puzzle fits them as a fallback that needs no third party.
  \item \textbf{A concrete security proof.} In the random-oracle model, a classical attacker derives the key even one oracle round early with probability at most about $2^{-117}$ for a 30-second lock against about the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network (\cref{sec:security}). Turning rounds into seconds needs one hardware parameter, the fastest time for one chained SHA-256, which we set at 3.5\,ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published design that we know of.
  \item \textbf{Practical costs.} A 30-second puzzle, enough when the order is fixed within a slot, takes about 21\,s to build on a laptop CPU and can be built before the transaction exists (\cref{sec:choosing-t} chooses the delay per use case). A sender that keeps a stock can use single-chain puzzles, which add 152 bytes to a transaction (\cref{sec:costs}).
  \item \textbf{An analysis of the trade-offs.} The puzzle guarantees eventual disclosure, not timely execution. Withholding can still delay or cancel execution, so a deposit forfeited on a missed reveal, which the reveal deadline makes observable, must deter it. A malformed puzzle is not detected, since no efficient check is known, so the guarantee covers senders that build their puzzles honestly, including those that later go offline or change their minds, but not a sender that plans to withhold from the start. A time lock also reveals transactions that are never included; we argue that this holds for every design whose fallback opening is input-free, and show how validity windows, private submission and a committee limit it (\cref{sec:tradeoffs}). We compare \leanseal with threshold, TEE, hybrid and delay-encryption designs (\cref{sec:comparison}).
  \item \textbf{An implementation}: a specified format with a Rust implementation, a second implementation in Python, test vectors and benchmarks, and a prototype encrypted mempool on an EVM chain (\cref{sec:poc})~\cite{leanSealRepo}. In the prototype, a sandwich attack costs a plaintext swap 9.25\%, while the same swap, sealed, executes at the fair price, because the pool refuses direct trades from the swap's submission until its batch executes, including a trade placed with a key read from a pending reveal; a withheld key is forced open.
\end{enumerate}

Neither the chained-hash puzzle nor the use of timed commitments against front-running is new (\cref{sec:related}). We are not aware of a prior design that uses a hash-only timed commitment as the fallback for self-decryption, without a committee. Beyond the design, our contributions are a concrete bound for an exact encoding, measured costs against an explicit attacker, and an analysis of what such a fallback can and cannot provide in an encrypted mempool.
