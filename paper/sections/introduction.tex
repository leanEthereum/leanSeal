\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In an encrypted mempool, block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run or sandwich a transaction based on its contents. The hard question is who opens the transactions afterwards. There are three answers in current designs, and each has a cost.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A committee} holds a threshold decryption key and opens what was ordered, as in Shutter~\cite{Shutter}. Privacy then rests on no threshold of its members colluding, and liveness on enough of them being online.
  \item \textbf{A trusted execution environment} opens transactions inside hardware whose vendor and side channels must be trusted.
  \item \textbf{The sender} opens its own transaction after the ordering, as in sealed transactions and the self-decryption path of LUCID~\cite{LUCID}. No one else is trusted, but the sender sees the ordering first and can withhold its key when the ordering does not suit it: a free option.
\end{itemize}
Hybrid designs~\cite{WM25} let either the sender or a committee open, which removes the free option while the committee is online and the committee's liveness problem while the sender is honest. Their bad case remains a dishonest sender while the committee is offline.

\leanseal removes the free option without any committee. The sender opens its own transaction in the normal case, but it attaches a time-lock puzzle that anyone can solve to recover the key after a fixed amount of sequential computation, about 30 seconds on Ethereum. Withholding the key therefore delays a transaction but cannot cancel or hide it. This is a timed commitment~\cite{BN00}. The puzzle uses only SHA-256: it needs no trusted setup, no committee and no hardware trust, and the idealized version is secure against quantum openers. The price is the sender's work. The puzzle is the chained-hash puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan~\cite{MMV11}, in which the sender computes as many hashes as an opener, only in parallel, and they proved that no hash-only puzzle can do better. This paper asks whether that price is affordable for sealing transactions, and what an encrypted mempool built on it can and cannot provide.

\paragraph{Our results.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A design.} We describe \leanseal as a decryption path for an encrypted mempool: self-decryption with a time-lock fallback (\cref{sec:mempool}). It puts on chain the same data as LUCID and the hybrid design, a ciphertext and a hash of its key, so it fits them as a path that needs no third party.
  \item \textbf{A concrete security proof.} In the random-oracle model, a classical attacker derives the key even one oracle round early with probability about $2^{-117}$ for a 30-second lock against the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network (\cref{sec:security}). Turning rounds into seconds needs one hardware number, the fastest time for one chained SHA-256, which we price at 3.5\,ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published design we found.
  \item \textbf{Affordable costs.} A 30-second puzzle, enough when the order is fixed within a slot, takes about 21\,s to build on a laptop CPU and can be built before the transaction exists (\cref{sec:choosing-t} chooses the delay per use case). A sender that keeps a stock can use single-chain puzzles, which add 152 bytes to a transaction (\cref{sec:costs}).
  \item \textbf{An honest account of the trade-offs.} A time lock also reveals transactions that are never included; we argue that this holds for every design whose fallback opening needs no secret, and show how validity windows, private submission and a committee limit it. Withholding still has some value, so a deposit carries the economics, and the time lock makes it enforceable (\cref{sec:tradeoffs}). We compare \leanseal with threshold, TEE, hybrid and delay-encryption designs (\cref{sec:comparison}).
  \item \textbf{An implementation}: a specified format with a Rust implementation, a second implementation in Python, test vectors and benchmarks, and a prototype encrypted mempool on an EVM chain in which a sealed swap escapes a sandwich that costs its plaintext version 9.25\% and a withheld key is forced open (\cref{sec:poc})~\cite{leanSealRepo}.
\end{enumerate}
Neither the puzzle nor its implementation is new in itself (\cref{sec:related}). What is new is a concrete bound for an exact encoding, measured costs against an explicit attacker, and its analysis as an encrypted mempool.
