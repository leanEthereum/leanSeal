\section{Weak Points and Mitigations}\label{sec:tradeoffs}\label{sec:mitigations}

\subsection{Excluded transactions are revealed too}\label{sec:noninclusion}

A time lock opens whether or not its transaction is included. If a builder receives a sealed transaction and leaves it out, the builder, or anyone else holding it, can read it after the delay. A threshold committee that decrypts only included transactions keeps excluded ones private, and that can matter: an excluded transaction can still reveal an intent to trade.

This is not specific to \leanseal. Call a fallback opening \emph{input-free} if it takes only the sealed transaction and data that existed when it was sealed.

\begin{claim}[informal]\label{claim:noninclusion}
In any scheme whose fallback opening is input-free, whoever can open an included sealed transaction without the sender can open it equally well when it is not included.
\end{claim}

Inclusion is not among the opening's inputs, so its run cannot depend on it. Revealing a transaction if and only if it is included therefore needs a party that can refuse to open, such as a committee, a TEE or the sender, or an opening that takes a proof of inclusion as input, as witness encryption with a blockchain clock does~\cite{LJKW18}, which is far from practical. The leak is also the price of the guarantee: a puzzle protects against a silent sender only because it has left the sender's hands before the ordering is fixed. Four measures make the leak later, smaller and less useful.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{It is late.} An excluded transaction opens a delay after exposure, not at exposure as in today's public mempool; most front-running value is gone by then.
  \item \textbf{Expiry.} A short validity window in the signed transaction means that by the time an excluded transaction can be read, nobody can execute it. What leaks is the intent. The sender can also cancel early by spending the nonce.
  \item \textbf{Fewer holders.} Sent only to the parties that can include it in the current slot, the builder or the inclusion-list committee, rather than gossiped, an excluded transaction is readable only by them, and exposure starts close to $\torder$. Broadcasting only a hash of the sealed transaction and publishing the rest once its slot is reserved~\cite{Naraghi26} has the same effect.
  \item \textbf{A committee.} Encrypting under a key derived from both a \leanseal key and a committee's key keeps excluded transactions private while the committee is honest (\cref{sec:composition}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Withholding delays but does not cancel}\label{sec:withholding}

With commit--reveal alone, the sender holds a free option: reveal if the ordering suits it, stay silent otherwise. The time lock turns silence into delay, so withholding cannot hide a transaction forever, and nobody can suppress a transaction by withholding a key. But execution cannot wait at the committed position for minutes of solving without stalling every later transaction. A late-opened transaction must execute later or be dropped, and with a tight deadline, a late execution that reverts is a cancellation. The economic deterrent must therefore come from a deposit, which the time lock makes enforceable.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A deposit forfeited on a missed reveal.} A missed reveal is visible on chain. The deposit is a plaintext field, since the protocol cannot see the transaction's value; LUCID's partly refunded top-of-block fee~\cite{LUCID} is one such rule.
  \item \textbf{The option is small.} During the reveal window the sender sees the ordering and the market, but not the other sealed transactions. Its option is roughly an at-the-money option over the window, worth about $0.4\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ of the traded value: about 0.015\%, or \$1.50 on a \$10,000 trade, for an asset with 60\% annual volatility and a 12-second window. A modest deposit covers it for small and medium trades; a large trade behind a small deposit keeps a residual option. If a late-opened transaction may still execute, the option runs until the forced opening instead: about 0.03\% for a one-minute fallback and 0.09\% for seven minutes, and the deposit should be sized for that window.
  \item \textbf{The last revealer.} Reveals should be collected until the deadline and published together, so that no sender decides after seeing the others.
\end{itemize}
Applications in which order within a batch does not matter avoid the problem. In a sealed-bid or uniform-price batch auction, settlement can wait until every bid is open, so a withheld bid is forced open and still binds its bidder. This is the classical use of timed commitments~\cite{BN00}, and the setting where \leanseal alone suffices.

\paragraph{Malformed puzzles.}
A sender could build a puzzle whose key matches $C$ but whose chains lead elsewhere. Under the deposit rule this needs no separate treatment: the penalty is triggered by the missed reveal, which is observable, not by the failed solve, which is not cheap to verify. What is lost is the guarantee that such a transaction becomes public. A protocol that needs that guarantee must check puzzles, and a proof much cheaper to check than the chains themselves is an open problem (\cref{sec:open}).

\subsection{Other requirements}\label{sec:protocol}\label{sec:admission}\label{sec:reorgs}\label{sec:solvers}

\paragraph{Finality.}
The deadline $\torder$ is when the position can no longer change. Today an Ethereum block can be reorganized until it is finalized, about 13 minutes later, far beyond a one-minute lock; after a reorganization, a revealed transaction can be front-run in the replacement block. A protocol can accept this once a block has its attestations, so that a reorganization degrades only the affected transactions to today's exposure. With the faster finality planned for lean consensus, $\torder$ shrinks to seconds and a one-minute lock covers it with a wide margin.

\paragraph{Admission.}
A sealed transaction cannot be checked before it is opened, so, as in other encrypted mempools, its plaintext envelope is signed and checked as usual and its fee is charged whether or not the opened transaction is valid. The number of open puzzles per sender should be capped.

\paragraph{Solvers.}
Opening a silent sender's transaction within about the delay needs a chip as fast as the assumed attacker; a laptop core takes 6.7 minutes. Even if every one of the about 265 transactions per slot in our mainnet sample needed it, that would keep about 1,300 attacker-speed circuits or 8,800 laptop cores busy. With deposits, silent senders are rare. A published key can be copied, so the reward cannot go to whoever posts it first: a commit--reveal of the key bound to the solver's address, or a designated solver per slot paid from the forfeited deposit, avoids the race.

\paragraph{Size and gas.}
\label{sec:size}Sealing adds 152 bytes with stockpiled single-chain puzzles and 512 with the reference puzzle. In 20 consecutive mainnet blocks (22 October 2026; 5,300 transactions; reproducible with \texttt{bench/eth-sample.py}), the median transaction was 179 bytes, so sealing makes it 1.8 or 3.9 times larger. As calldata at 16 gas per nonzero byte the added bytes cost about 2,400 or 8,200 gas, against 21,000 for a plain transfer, and under the 40-gas floor of EIP-7623 about 6,100 or 20,500. The ordering needs only the 32-byte hash of each sealed transaction; the rest only has to stay available until it is opened, and a blob, kept for about 18 days, is enough.

\paragraph{Sharing puzzles.}
One puzzle can cover all of a sender's transactions that should open together. It cannot be shared with unrelated senders: everyone who encrypts under a key knows it. Sharing the cost of opening across senders is delay encryption, which hashes cannot provide when the setup is cheap (\cref{sec:limits}). A sender without capable hardware can instead outsource building to several services and encrypt under a hash of all their keys, which stays secret if any one service is honest; no service can prevent the opening.
