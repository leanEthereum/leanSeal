\section{Weak Points and Mitigations}\label{sec:tradeoffs}\label{sec:mitigations}

\subsection{Excluded transactions are revealed too}\label{sec:noninclusion}

A time lock opens whether or not its transaction is included. If a builder receives a sealed transaction and leaves it out, the builder, or anyone else holding it, can read it after the delay. A threshold committee that decrypts only included transactions keeps excluded ones private, and that can matter: an excluded transaction can still reveal an intent to trade.

This is not specific to \leanseal. Call a fallback opening \emph{input-free} if it takes only the sealed transaction and auxiliary data that is available whether or not the transaction is included, with no later input or secret release that depends on inclusion.

\begin{claim}[informal]\label{claim:noninclusion}
In any scheme whose fallback opening is input-free, whoever can open an included sealed transaction without the sender can open it equally well when it is not included.
\end{claim}

Inclusion is not among the opening's inputs, so its run cannot depend on it. Revealing a transaction if and only if it is included therefore needs either a party that can refuse to open, such as a committee, a TEE or the sender, or an opening that takes a later inclusion witness as input, as witness encryption with a blockchain clock does~\cite{LJKW18}. The second class is outside the claim; we know of no practical construction in it, and the later input must come from some party or from consensus. Naraghi draws the same three-way distinction among the inputs of a forced opening~\cite{Naraghi26}. The leak is also the price of the guarantee: a puzzle protects against a silent sender only because it has left the sender's hands before the ordering is fixed. Four measures make the leak later, smaller and less useful.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{It is late.} An excluded transaction opens a delay after exposure, not at exposure as in today's public mempool; most front-running value is gone by then.
  \item \textbf{Expiry.} A short validity window in the signed transaction means that by the time an excluded transaction can be read, nobody can execute it. What leaks is the intent. The sender can also cancel early by spending the nonce.
  \item \textbf{Fewer holders.} Sent only to the parties that can include it in the current slot, the builder or the inclusion-list committee, rather than gossiped, an excluded transaction is readable only by them, and exposure starts close to $\torder$. Broadcasting only a hash of the sealed transaction and publishing the rest once its slot is reserved~\cite{Herold26,Naraghi26} also starts exposure close to $\torder$, and a transaction whose commitment is never included is never exposed; but the sender can then decline to publish, which the deposit must cover (\cref{sec:comparison}).
  \item \textbf{A committee.} Encrypting under a key derived from both a \leanseal key and a committee's key keeps excluded transactions private while the committee is honest (\cref{sec:composition}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Withholding: disclosure is forced, execution is not}\label{sec:withholding}

With commit--reveal alone, the sender holds a free option: reveal if the ordering suits it, stay silent otherwise. The time lock turns silence into delay, so withholding cannot keep a well-formed puzzle's transaction hidden. But execution cannot wait at the committed position for minutes of solving without stalling every later transaction. A late-opened transaction must execute later or be dropped, and with a tight deadline, a late execution that reverts is a cancellation. For a well-formed puzzle, withholding cannot prevent eventual disclosure, but it can still cause late execution or cancellation, and a deposit must deter that choice. The reveal deadline makes a missed reveal observable; the time lock separately guarantees disclosure.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A deposit forfeited on a missed reveal.} A missed reveal is visible on chain. The deposit is a plaintext field, since the protocol cannot see the transaction's value; LUCID's partly refunded top-of-block fee~\cite{LUCID} is one such rule. The penalty must be irrecoverable: the sender knows its own key, so any part of the deposit paid to whoever publishes the key is a part the sender can claim back. A forfeitable fee is also much weaker against an attacker with two or more commitments ahead of its victim (fee inversion~\cite{Naraghi26}, \cref{sec:comparison}). Capping at one the commitments that may execute ahead of a transaction, or making release decisions blind (below), avoids this.
  \item \textbf{The value of the option.} During the reveal window the sender sees the ordering and the market, but not the other sealed transactions. Its option is roughly an at-the-money option over the window, worth about $0.4\,\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$ of the traded value: about 0.015\%, or \$1.50 on a \$10,000 trade, for an asset with 60\% annual volatility and a 12-second window. This is the value of an unpenalized option in a volatility-only model, not a sufficient deposit: a deposit charged only when the option is exercised raises the exercise threshold but leaves a residual option (a deposit equal to the option's value still leaves more than half of it), and the ordering itself carries information the model omits. Sizing the deposit needs a target exercise probability or a bound on the gain from withholding~\cite{Naraghi26}. If a late-opened transaction may still execute, the option runs until the forced opening instead: about 0.02\% if fast solvers open the reference puzzle in 30 seconds and 0.06\% for the 3.3 minutes one laptop core takes, and the deposit must be sized for that window.
  \item \textbf{The last revealer.} A sender that sees the others' reveals before its own deadline decides with more information. Releasing reveals simultaneously is an additional protocol requirement; a party that merely collects plaintext keys could leak them early. Blind simultaneous release~\cite{Naraghi26} is one mechanism: right after ordering, each releaser, here each sender, escrows its key under a timed pad that outlasts a window of one or two seconds, so every decision to reveal is fixed before any key is public. It needs a committee to fix the set of timely escrows, funded fast solvers and a slashable deposit, and escrows by end users are still open (\cref{sec:comparison}).
\end{itemize}
Applications in which order within a batch does not matter avoid the problem. In a sealed-bid or uniform-price batch auction, settlement can wait until every bid is open, so a withheld bid is forced open and still binds its bidder, provided puzzles are checked to be well formed and bidders' funds are escrowed. This is the classical use of timed commitments~\cite{BN00}, and \leanseal suffices on its own here once puzzles can be checked.

\paragraph{Malformed puzzles.}
A sender could build a puzzle whose key matches $C$ but whose chains lead elsewhere. Under the deposit rule this needs no separate treatment: the penalty is triggered by the missed reveal, which is observable, not by the failed solve, which is not cheap to verify. What is lost is the guarantee that such a transaction becomes public. A protocol that needs that guarantee must check puzzles, and a proof much cheaper to check than the chains themselves is an open problem (\cref{sec:open}).

\subsection{Other requirements}\label{sec:protocol}\label{sec:admission}\label{sec:reorgs}\label{sec:solvers}

\paragraph{Finality.}
The deadline $\torder$ is when the position can no longer change. Today an Ethereum block can be reorganized until it is finalized, about 13 minutes later, far beyond a 30-second lock; after a reorganization, a revealed transaction can be front-run in the replacement block. A protocol can accept this once a block has its attestations, so that a reorganization degrades only the affected transactions to today's exposure. With the faster finality planned for lean consensus, $\torder$ shrinks to seconds and a 30-second lock covers it with a wide margin.

\paragraph{Admission.}
A sealed transaction cannot be checked before it is opened, so, as in other encrypted mempools, its plaintext envelope is signed and checked as usual and its fee is charged whether or not the opened transaction is valid. The number of open puzzles per sender should be capped.

\paragraph{Solvers.}
Opening a silent sender's transaction within about the delay needs a chip as fast as the assumed attacker; a laptop core takes 3.3 minutes for the reference puzzle. Even if every one of the about 265 transactions per slot in our mainnet sample needed it, that would keep about 660 attacker-speed circuits or 4,400 laptop cores busy. With deposits, silent senders are rare. A published key can be copied, so the reward cannot go to whoever posts it first: a commit--reveal of the key bound to the solver's address, or a designated solver per slot, avoids the race. The reward cannot come from the sender's own deposit, which the sender could then claim itself; it must be paid from fees or a protocol pool.

\paragraph{Size and gas.}
\label{sec:size}Sealing adds 152 bytes with stockpiled single-chain puzzles and 512 with the reference puzzle. In 20 consecutive mainnet blocks (22 October 2026; 5,300 transactions; reproducible with \texttt{bench/eth-sample.py}), the median transaction was 179 bytes, so sealing makes it 1.8 or 3.9 times larger. As calldata at 16 gas per nonzero byte the added bytes cost about 2,400 or 8,200 gas, against 21,000 for a plain transfer, and under the 40-gas floor of EIP-7623 about 6,100 or 20,500. The ordering needs only the 32-byte hash of each sealed transaction; the rest only has to stay available until it is opened, and a blob, kept for about 18 days, is enough.

\paragraph{Sharing puzzles.}
One puzzle can cover all of a sender's transactions that should open together. It cannot be shared with unrelated senders: everyone who encrypts under a key knows it. Sharing the cost of opening across senders is delay encryption, which hashes cannot provide when the setup is cheap (\cref{sec:limits}). A sender without capable hardware can instead outsource building to several services and encrypt under a hash of all their keys, which stays secret if any one service is honest; no service can prevent the opening.
