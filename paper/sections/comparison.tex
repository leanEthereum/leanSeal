The table exhibits a trade-off. Every design in the table that keeps excluded transactions private has a party that can refuse to open, and that party is also the one that can fail to open; \cref{claim:noninclusion} says this is forced for input-free fallbacks. Among the designs compared here with an input-free fallback, \leanseal is the only one with no setup and a plausible post-quantum argument. Relative to the other designs, it has three costs: bytes (152 to 512 per transaction, against a 32-byte commitment), the sender's computation, and minutes rather than seconds for a forced opening.

Post-quantum security is a further constraint on the committee designs, not a reason to rule them out, but none of them meets it in practice today. Batched threshold encryption, which lets a committee open a batch with communication independent of its size while excluded transactions stay private, is built from pairings~\cite{CGPW24}. The lattice-based threshold batch IBE of Boneh, Laufer and Tas~\cite{BLT25} is a post-quantum candidate, without reported sizes. Tree-BTE~\cite{ADGPR26} can be instantiated from ring LWE; its partial implementation of that version uses about 0.89\,MB per ciphertext for a batch of 1,024, against 152 to 512 bytes for \leanseal. Without batching, lattice threshold decryption needs a share from each member for each transaction. A post-quantum committee can still back up \leanseal in the compositions of \cref{sec:composition}, where it carries only a fallback key.

DecryptChain~\cite{AK26} also removes the fixed committee. It keeps a decryption committee but opens its membership through public proof-of-work in each epoch, so that a compromised committee can be replaced and committed transactions are eventually decrypted. In \leanseal, no committee is involved: the fallback is the sender's own puzzle, which anyone can solve.

The closest work is Naraghi's analysis of reveal optionality~\cite{Naraghi26}, which appeared while this paper was being written. Three of its results bear on \leanseal.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A timing bound.} Its Theorem~2 covers every forced opening whose inputs are public at broadcast: privacy until the order is fixed and opening by the execution deadline are compatible only if the attacker is at most an explicit ratio of protocol times faster than the honest solvers, a ratio below two once a ciphertext waits a slot in a public mempool and must execute in the next. \leanseal is consistent with this because the sender's reveal is the timely path; the forced opening may finish minutes later, at the cost of late or no execution (\cref{sec:withholding}). With 200\,s from the fixed order to the execution deadline, about one laptop core's time for the reference puzzle, a 4-second ordering delay and a 12-second wait, the bound allows an attacker 13.5 times faster than honest solvers starting at broadcast, or 12.5 if they start once the order is fixed; our assumed attacker is 6.6 times faster than a laptop core.
  \item \textbf{Fee inversion.} Pricing a missed reveal with a forfeitable fee, as LUCID does, is much weaker when two commitments may execute ahead of the victim: the attacker can arrange to forfeit the large fee only when its guess about the victim is right, so once its best guess has probability above $2/(F+1)$, where $1/F$ is the share of the fee a revealed transaction pays, deterrence needs a fee of at least half its gain. This applies to \leanseal's deposit as well (\cref{sec:withholding}). Under BSR, below, the single-commitment fee suffices again, because every reveal decision is fixed before any key is public.
  \item \textbf{Blind simultaneous release (BSR).} BSR confines a time lock to the decision window. Right after the order is fixed, each releaser (a key publisher, a committee member, or a sender holding a fallback key) escrows its release value under a timed pad, a precomputed iterated hash, within a window of one or two seconds. After the window, values are revealed or forced open in time for the batch to execute at the top of the next payload, and a releaser whose escrow does not open its commitment is slashed. Privacy before ordering comes from the underlying scheme, not from the pad. On a ticket-first design with twelve-second slots, forced opening in time needs funded solvers within a factor of 3 to 3.5 of the attacker, or 7 to 8 with a one-second window.
\end{itemize}
BSR and \leanseal solve different problems. \leanseal keeps a transaction private before ordering without a committee or key publisher and guarantees that it is eventually disclosed; BSR makes release decisions simultaneous, which removes the option to decide after seeing others' reveals. They could compose if a \leanseal sender acted as its own releaser and escrowed $K$ in the BSR window, which would supply the simultaneous release that \leanseal leaves open (\cref{sec:withholding}); Naraghi lists such end-user escrows as open, because of the deposit's capital cost and the pad's precomputation. A \leanseal puzzle could serve as the pad: one that covers a 2-second window against our assumed attacker builds in about 1.4\,s on the M4 Air, which bears on the precomputation barrier, but opens in about 13\,s on one laptop core, so it still needs BSR's funded solvers.

Our analysis also supplies concrete figures that Naraghi's leaves open. His pad is a single hash chain whose sequentiality is taken from the random-oracle model, without a concrete bound; a \leanseal puzzle with $n = 1$ has this form, and \cref{thm:delay} gives it one. Our hardware model (\cref{sec:pricing}) bears on the hardware gap that he lists as open: against our assumed attacker, a factor of 3 to 3.5 requires honest solvers at 10 to 12\,ns per hash, faster than one Apple M4 core (23.2\,ns) or the Xeon core Naraghi measures (70\,ns) and close to the fastest published design (13.0\,ns); a factor of 7 to 8 admits a laptop core. These figures rest on our estimate, not on a measurement.

\subsection{Composing \leanseal with a threshold committee}\label{sec:composition}

A time lock and a committee fail in different ways: the time lock relies on a hardware estimate and leaks excluded transactions, and the committee relies on no threshold of its members colluding and on enough of them being online. The two can be combined in two ways. Let $K_{\mathrm{tl}}$ be a \leanseal key and $K_{\mathrm{th}}$ a key that a threshold committee releases for included transactions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{AND.} Encrypt under $\mathsf{H}(K_{\mathrm{tl}}, K_{\mathrm{th}})$. Reading the transaction needs both keys, so the contents stay hidden while the committee is honest \emph{or} the delay has not passed. With an honest committee, excluded transactions are never revealed. With a colluding committee, the puzzle still keeps the contents hidden for the delay, so the committee cannot read transactions before ordering them. Opening needs both the committee and the puzzle (or the sender's reveal), so a committee that goes offline blocks it.
  \item \textbf{OR.} Encrypt the transaction's key twice, once under each. Either key opens the transaction, so opening needs the committee \emph{or} the puzzle: a committee that withholds keys or goes offline cannot block the opening. Privacy needs the committee to be honest \emph{and} the delay not to have passed.
\end{itemize}
\Cref{tab:composition} summarizes this. In both, the sender's own reveal remains the fast path. The OR form has the structure of the hybrid designs of \cref{sec:design-space}, with \leanseal as a fallback that requires no trusted party; the AND form uses \leanseal as a floor for privacy under a committee.

\subsection{Settings where \leanseal is practical}\label{sec:realistic}

\leanseal is practical where three conditions hold: the order is fixed within seconds of exposure, a deposit is acceptable, and a delay of minutes is tolerable for a withholding sender's transaction. Three settings satisfy them.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{The self-decryption path of LUCID or a hybrid.} Here \leanseal serves as a fallback that requires no trusted party. In the OR form it removes the hybrid design's failure case, a withholding sender while the committee is offline, at the cost of 92 to 452 bytes of puzzle per transaction. Inside LUCID it uses only the puzzle, in the envelope's header; executing a transaction disclosed late needs a protocol rule LUCID does not have.
  \item \textbf{Sealed-bid and batch auctions.} Settlement can wait for every bid to open, so a withheld bid is forced open and still binds its bidder. With escrowed bids and the bond of \cref{sec:bond}, \leanseal suffices on its own (\cref{sec:auction}).
  \item \textbf{Rollups and chains with fast finality}, where a sequencer or a fast-finality protocol fixes the order within seconds, so a lock of 15 to 30 seconds leaves a wide margin.
\end{itemize}
It is impractical for transactions that wait several slots in a public mempool. The clock starts at exposure, so a two-slot wait exhausts a 30-second lock; broadcasting only a commitment and publishing the puzzle once the slot is reserved~\cite{Naraghi26}, or sending it only to the slot's builder, removes the wait. It is also impractical wherever excluded transactions must stay private and no committee is acceptable: an input-free fallback cannot do both (\cref{claim:noninclusion}), and openings that take later inclusion witnesses, which are outside this claim, are not practical today.
