\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We present \leanseal, a simple encrypted mempool construction that needs neither a committee nor a trusted setup, relying only on a hash function such as SHA-256. Encrypted mempools let block builders order transactions before reading them, which prevents front-running and sandwich attacks based on their contents. Existing designs either trust a committee to open the transactions afterwards, or let each sender open its own, in which case the sender can withhold its key when the ordering is unfavorable. In \leanseal, the sender opens its own transaction but also attaches the chained-hash time-lock puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (CRYPTO 2011), which anyone can solve to recover the key after a fixed amount of sequential computation. Withholding the key therefore cannot keep the transaction hidden, provided that it was sealed correctly.

For our exact encoding, we prove in the random-oracle model that even a classical attacker with about the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network recovers the key of a 30-second puzzle early with probability at most about $2^{-117}$. The delay rests on one hardware estimate, 3.5\,ns per chained SHA-256 for the fastest attacker; at 1.3\,ns, a 30-second puzzle opens in 11.2\,s, before a 12-second ordering deadline. Such a puzzle takes about 21\,s to build on a laptop, ahead of time, and adds 152 to 512 bytes to a transaction.

Two limitations follow from the time-lock fallback: a sender that withholds its key can still delay or cancel execution, which a deposit must deter, and transactions that are never included are eventually disclosed. We describe how a threshold committee can remove either one, depending on how its key is combined with the puzzle's. Since a sender could also seal incorrectly on purpose, we add a bond, forfeited when a forced opening is shown on chain to fail, if necessary by a bisection game, which charges strategic withholding but not honest senders that go offline. In a sealed-bid batch auction, a valid, escrowed bid with a well-formed puzzle then remains binding after a missed reveal, and withholding a malformed bid costs the bond as well as the deposit.
\end{abstract}
