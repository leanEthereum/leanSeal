\begin{abstract}
\noindent
An encrypted mempool lets block builders order transactions before they can read them. Someone must then open the transactions: a committee adds trust assumptions for both privacy and liveness, and a sender that opens its own transaction can withhold its key when the ordering does not suit it. We present \leanseal, in which the sender opens its own transaction but attaches a time-lock puzzle built from SHA-256 alone, so that anyone can open the transaction without the sender after about a minute of sequential computation. Withholding the key then delays a transaction but cannot cancel or hide it, with no committee and no trusted setup.

The puzzle is the chained-hash puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (CRYPTO 2011). For our encoding we prove, in the random-oracle model, that a classical attacker with the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network derives the key early with probability about $2^{-116}$ for a one-minute lock. Against an attacker who computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5\,ns, a one-minute puzzle costs the sender 42\,s on a laptop CPU, can be built before the transaction exists, and adds 152 to 512 bytes to the transaction; opening with the revealed key takes 12\,ms. We also work out what \leanseal cannot do. Like every time lock, it reveals excluded transactions too. We show how deposits, validity windows and composition with a committee mitigate its weak points, and compare it with threshold, TEE and hybrid designs.
