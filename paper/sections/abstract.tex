\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We present \leanseal, a simple encrypted mempool construction that needs neither a committee nor a trusted setup, relying only on a hash function such as SHA-256. Encrypted mempools let block builders order transactions before reading them, which prevents front-running and sandwich attacks based on their contents. Existing designs either trust a committee to open the transactions afterwards, or let each sender open its own, in which case the sender can withhold its key when the ordering is unfavorable. In \leanseal, the sender opens its own transaction but also attaches the chained-hash time-lock puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (CRYPTO 2011), which anyone can solve to recover the key after a fixed amount of sequential computation. Withholding the key therefore cannot keep the transaction hidden, provided that it was sealed correctly.

For our exact encoding, we prove in the random-oracle model that even a classical attacker with about the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network recovers the key of a 30-second puzzle early with probability at most about $2^{-117}$. The delay rests on one hardware estimate, 3.5\,ns per chained SHA-256 for the fastest attacker. Such a puzzle takes about 21\,s to build on a laptop, ahead of time, and adds 152 to 512 bytes to a transaction.

\leanseal also combines with a threshold committee that releases keys for included transactions. If reading a transaction needs both the committee's key and the puzzle's, transactions that are never included stay private while the committee is honest, whereas the puzzle alone discloses them after the delay. If either key suffices, the committee can open transactions at once, so a sender can no longer delay or cancel execution by withholding its key, which otherwise only a deposit deters. We also add a bond that charges a sender for a puzzle sealed incorrectly on purpose, and apply \leanseal to sealed-bid batch auctions.
\end{abstract}
