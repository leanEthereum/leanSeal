\section{Sealing Transactions with \leanseal}\label{sec:mempool}

This section treats the puzzle as a black box and describes how it backs an encrypted mempool. The sender runs $\Build$ to get a puzzle $Z$, which contains a commitment $C$, and a key $K$. Anyone can check a claimed key against $C$ with one hash. Anyone can also recover $K$ from $Z$ alone with $N$ sequential hashes, which takes at least $T = N\ell$ seconds on the fastest hardware. \Cref{sec:construction,sec:security} construct the puzzle and prove this.

\subsection{The flow}\label{sec:flow}

\leanseal is used as a timed commitment~\cite{BN00}; \cref{fig:commit-reveal} shows the flow.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Submit.} The sender takes a puzzle from its stock and encrypts its signed transaction under $K$. It sends the sealed transaction together with a plaintext envelope: the account that pays, a nonce, a gas limit, a fee and a deposit (\cref{sec:admission}).
  \item \textbf{Order.} The builder orders sealed transactions without reading them. The ordering commits to the hash of each whole sealed transaction, not just to $C$: once $K$ is known, anyone could seal a different transaction under the same puzzle.
  \item \textbf{Reveal.} Once the ordering is fixed, the sender publishes $K$. Nodes check it against $C$, decrypt in about 12\,ms and execute the transaction in its committed position. Nobody solves anything.
  \item \textbf{Fallback.} If the sender stays silent past the reveal deadline, its deposit is forfeited, and anyone can solve the puzzle and publish $K$. A well-formed puzzle therefore opens without its sender.
\end{enumerate}

The data this puts on chain, a ciphertext and a hash of its key, is the shape that recent Ethereum designs already use. In LUCID (EIP-8184)~\cite{LUCID}, a sealed transaction's ticket carries a key commitment and a ciphertext hash, and the key is released by a key publisher of the sender's choice, possibly the sender itself, with a fee forfeited on failure to reveal. In the hybrid design of Wagner and Ma~\cite{WM25}, the sender puts $\mathrm{Enc}_k(\mathit{tx})$ and $\mathsf{H}(k)$ on chain and gives a committee a threshold encryption of $k$, so that either the sender or the committee can open. \leanseal fits both as a decryption path that needs no third party: the sender is its own key publisher, and the puzzle replaces the committee as the party that opens when the sender does not.

The reveal should be due as soon as the ordering is fixed. With enshrined proposer--builder separation, the builder commits to an ordered list of sealed transactions at the start of a slot, and senders reveal before the payload deadline of the same slot, as in LUCID. A short window shortens the delay the puzzle must provide and limits what a sender learns before deciding whether to reveal (\cref{sec:withholding}).

\subsection{The timing condition}\label{sec:timing}

The attacker's clock starts at \emph{exposure}, $\texp$, the first moment anyone but the sender holds the puzzle. The deadline $\torder$ is the moment the transaction's position can no longer change. The contents stay hidden until the deadline if
\[
  \torder - \texp < N \cdot \lattacker,
\]
with a margin for propagation. A forced opening finishes $N \cdot \lsolver$ after a solver starts, which on a laptop core is minutes after the deadline. If the attacker is $k$ times faster than assumed, the delay shrinks $k$-fold; once it falls below $\torder - \texp$, the contents can be read before the order is fixed. Even then the result is no worse than today's public mempool, where everyone reads a transaction as soon as it is broadcast.

\subsection{Choosing the delay}\label{sec:choosing-t}

The delay should be the smallest that covers the time from exposure to the fixed order, $\torder - \texp$. Building, forced opening, the solvers' load and the window in which a silent sender holds an option all grow linearly with $T$; only the size does not. The attacker speed already carries a margin of about 3.7 over the fastest published design (\cref{sec:pricing}), so we take $T$ about twice the time to fix the order. \Cref{tab:choosing-t} applies this to the settings of \cref{sec:realistic}. We use $T = 30$\,s as the reference: it covers a puzzle that is sent only to the slot's builder, or published after a commitment, and ordered within one 12-second slot.

The last row is why \leanseal needs an order that is fixed early. Waiting for today's finality makes every cost thirty times larger than in the reference setting, so a protocol either accepts that a reorganization exposes revealed transactions (\cref{sec:reorgs}) or relies on fast finality, which brings the delay back to the reference range. Public gossip is affordable for a wait of a slot or two but not more, which is why the puzzle should not wait in a public mempool.

\subsection{A proof of concept}\label{sec:poc}

We implemented this flow as a contract on any chain with EIP-7702, with off-chain sender, keeper and solver roles~\cite{leanSealRepo}. The block that includes a sealed transaction fixes its position. The sender reveals its key within two blocks, and anyone then executes the slot's revealed transactions in order: the contract checks each key against its commitment and decrypts the envelope, both with SHA-256 only, and calls the sender's account, which delegates to the contract for this. A missed reveal forfeits the deposit to whoever later opens the transaction from its puzzle. On a local chain, a plaintext swap was sandwiched by a searcher watching the mempool and lost 9.25\% against the fair price. The same swap, sealed, executed at the fair price. With its key withheld, it was opened by a solver 19.5\,s later and executed late. Submitting costs about 199{,}000 gas, revealing 74{,}000 and executing a swap with its decryption about 150{,}000. Ordering in a contract inherits the chain's reorganizations, so the prototype fits chains with fast finality; inside LUCID, the puzzle would travel in the ciphertext envelope's scheme-specific header.
