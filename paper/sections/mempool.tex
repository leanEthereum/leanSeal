\section{Sealing Transactions with \leanseal}\label{sec:mempool}

This section treats the puzzle as a black box and describes how it supports an encrypted mempool. The sender runs $\Build$ to obtain a puzzle $Z$, which contains a commitment $C$, and a key $K$. Anyone can check a claimed key against $C$ with one hash. Anyone can also recover $K$ from $Z$, without the sender, with $N$ sequential hashes, which takes at least $T = N\ell$ seconds on the fastest hardware. \Cref{sec:construction,sec:security} construct the puzzle and prove this. \Cref{tab:props} lists the properties an encrypted mempool may want and what \leanseal provides; no known design provides all of them, and \cref{sec:comparison} compares the alternatives.

\subsection{The flow}\label{sec:flow}

\leanseal is used as a timed commitment~\cite{BN00}; \cref{fig:commit-reveal} shows the flow.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Submit.} The sender takes a puzzle from its stock and encrypts its signed transaction under $K$. It sends the sealed transaction together with a plaintext envelope: the account that pays, a nonce, a gas limit, a fee and a deposit (\cref{sec:admission}).
  \item \textbf{Order.} The builder orders sealed transactions without reading them. The ordering commits to the hash of each whole sealed transaction, not only to $C$: once $K$ is known, anyone could seal a different transaction under the same puzzle.
  \item \textbf{Reveal.} Once the ordering is fixed, the sender publishes $K$. Nodes check it against $C$, decrypt in milliseconds and execute the transaction in its committed position. No puzzle is solved.
  \item \textbf{Fallback.} If the sender has not revealed $K$ by the reveal deadline, its deposit is forfeited, and anyone can solve the puzzle and publish $K$. We call a puzzle \emph{well formed} if its chains lead to the key committed in $C$ and its transaction is encrypted under that key. A well-formed puzzle therefore opens without its sender, but its transaction can only run late or not at all.
\end{enumerate}


A ciphertext bound to a commitment to its key is the structure that recent Ethereum designs already use. In LUCID (EIP-8184)~\cite{LUCID}, a sealed transaction's ticket goes on chain and carries a commitment $\mathsf{keccak256}(k_{\mathrm{dem}})$ to the key and a hash of the ciphertext envelope, which itself travels off chain. The key is released by a key publisher of the sender's choice, possibly the sender itself, and the publication fee is paid only when decryption succeeds. In the hybrid design of Wagner and Ma~\cite{WM25}, the sender puts $\mathrm{Enc}_k(\mathit{tx})$ and $\mathsf{H}(k)$ on chain and gives a committee a threshold encryption of $k$, so that either the sender or the committee can open. \leanseal fits both as a fallback that needs no third party: the sender is its own key publisher, and the puzzle replaces the committee as the party that discloses the key when the sender does not.

Inside LUCID, only the puzzle is used. It travels in the envelope's opaque header with $K$ as $k_{\mathrm{dem}}$; its SHA-256 commitment $C$ is separate from the ticket's keccak commitment, and the cipher stays ChaCha20-Poly1305. The puzzle provides eventual disclosure only: executing a transaction whose key is disclosed minutes late needs an additional protocol rule, which LUCID does not have.

The reveal should be due as soon as the ordering is fixed. In LUCID, the builder's bid commits to the tickets of slot $n$, keys are released once the beacon block carrying the bid is attested and must be seen before the payload-timeliness committee votes, and the decrypted transactions execute at the top of block $n+1$, before any ordinary transaction. A short window shortens the delay the puzzle must provide and limits what a sender learns before deciding whether to reveal (\cref{sec:withholding}).

\subsection{The timing condition}\label{sec:timing}

The attacker's clock starts at \emph{exposure}, $\texp$, the first moment anyone but the sender holds the puzzle. The deadline $\torder$ is the moment the transaction's position can no longer change. The contents stay hidden until the deadline if
\[
  \torder - \texp < N \cdot \lattacker,
\]
with a margin for propagation. A forced opening finishes $N \cdot \lsolver$ after a solver starts, which on a laptop core is minutes after the deadline.

If the attacker is $k$ times faster than assumed, the delay shrinks $k$-fold. Once it falls below $\torder - \texp$, the contents can be read before the order is fixed: privacy before ordering is lost, and content-dependent MEV becomes possible again for any party that can solve the puzzle in time.

\subsection{Choosing the delay}\label{sec:choosing-t}

The delay should be the smallest that covers the time from exposure to the fixed order, $\torder - \texp$. Building, forced opening, the solvers' load and the window in which a withholding sender holds an option all grow linearly with $T$; only the size does not. The attacker speed already carries a margin of about 3.7 over the fastest published design (\cref{sec:pricing}), so we take $T$ about twice the time to fix the order, with a floor of 15\,s and rounded up. \Cref{tab:choosing-t} applies this to the settings of \cref{sec:realistic}. We use $T = 30$\,s as the reference: it covers a puzzle that is sent only to the slot's builder, or published after a commitment, and ordered within one 12-second slot.

The last row shows why \leanseal needs an order that is fixed early. Waiting for today's finality makes every cost sixty times larger than in the reference setting, so a protocol either accepts that a reorganization exposes revealed transactions (\cref{sec:reorgs}) or relies on fast finality, which brings the delay back to the reference range. Public gossip remains affordable for a wait of one or two slots but not longer, so the puzzle should not wait in a public mempool.

\subsection{A proof of concept}\label{sec:poc}

We implemented this flow as an EVM contract, using EIP-7702 and transient storage, with off-chain sender, keeper and solver roles~\cite{leanSealRepo}. Each block's sealed transactions form a batch, and batches execute strictly in order from one queue. On submission, the contract checks each envelope's structure against the specification and caps the gas per transaction and per batch. The sender reveals its key within two blocks; anyone then executes the batch in order. The contract checks each key against its commitment, decrypts the envelope with SHA-256 only and calls the sender's account. Only accounts delegated to the contract's own account code may submit, and the delegation is checked again before the call, so the call that runs is the committed one. The queue advances past a transaction only if the executor supplied its full gas, and a failing or redelegated account cannot block it.

Two further rules make the committed order binding for a market. First, a guarded pool refuses direct trades from the submission of a sealed transaction until its batch has executed. A key is public as soon as its reveal is pending, before the reveal executes, so a lock that started at the reveal would let whoever reads the pending key trade ahead of it. While sealed transactions keep arriving, the pool therefore trades only through them. Second, sealed calls are restricted to the pool, so a sealed transaction ordered ahead of another has parameters fixed before any reveal. Otherwise it could read state that the attacker wrote after the reveals and adapt to the victim's plaintext. General programmable transactions need execution immediately after key release with nothing in between, which only a protocol such as LUCID provides.

A missed reveal burns most of the deposit: the solver who posts the key receives a smaller bounty, which the sender, who knows its key, could claim itself, so the net penalty is the deposit minus the bounty. The deposit prices only the missed reveal: a sender that reveals on time and then revokes its approval or moves its funds makes its own trade fail without penalty, a cancellation that also only immediate execution removes. A forced opening publishes the key and places the transaction at the end of the queue, so it never trades ahead of a waiting transaction; its key is public before its position is fixed, so it has no protection against front-running.

On a local chain with 2-second blocks, a searcher watching the mempool sandwiched a plaintext swap, which lost 9.25\% against the fair price. The same swap, sealed, executed at the fair price: the searcher read the key from the pending reveal, decrypted the swap and placed a trade ahead of the reveal in the same block, and the pool rejected it. With its key withheld, a demonstration puzzle of $2^{29}$ steps, 1/16 of the reference work, was solved on one core in 13\,s, and the swap executed late.

The current prototype has not been audited, and its puzzles are not checked for solvability. Its keeper splits a batch into transactions within the gas cap of EIP-7825. In the contract's unit tests, with a 128-byte test puzzle and 256-byte swaps, submitting costs 192{,}000 to 268{,}000 gas, revealing about 74{,}500, executing a batch of two swaps about 356{,}000, and a forced opening about 127{,}000 to publish the key and 217{,}000 to execute late. These are contract-call costs; calldata and intrinsic costs come on top and grow with the envelope.
