Time-Lock Encryption from Hashes Alone

A chained-hash puzzle for sealing transactions, what it costs, what hashes cannot do, and an implementation

Abstract

An encrypted mempool lets block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run based on the transactions' contents. Each transaction must then open once it is ordered, without a committee that holds a decryption key and without letting its sender keep it sealed. A time-lock puzzle provides this. Efficient time-lock puzzles use repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, which needs a trusted setup or is broken by quantum computers. Puzzles built from a hash function avoid both, but the sender must compute as many hashes as the opener, only in parallel, and Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (CRYPTO 2011) showed that this cost is unavoidable. We ask whether it is affordable for sealing a transaction.

We present chainseal, which instantiates their chained-hash puzzle with SHA-256, with a specification and a Rust implementation. In the random-oracle model, we bound the chance that a classical attacker derives the key even one oracle round early: against the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network, it is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock with 24-byte links. Against an attacker who computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published design we surveyed, a one-minute puzzle costs the sender 42.1 s on the CPU of an Apple M4 laptop and adds 512 bytes to the transaction, and it can be built before the transaction exists. Used behind commit–reveal, it makes a silent sender's transaction openable by anyone: for a well-formed puzzle, withholding the key delays the transaction but cannot cancel it.

1. Introduction

In an encrypted mempool, block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run based on the transactions' contents. The hard part is opening the transactions afterwards. A committee can hold a decryption key, but then secrecy rests on no threshold of its members colluding. The sender can reveal its own key after ordering, but then the sender sees the ordering first and can withhold the key when the ordering does not suit it. A time lock avoids both: anyone can open the transaction without its sender once a fixed amount of computation has been done, and nobody can do that computation much faster by buying more machines. Combined with the sender's own reveal, this is a timed commitment (Boneh and Naor 2000): the sender still opens its transaction in the normal case, and the puzzle only takes away its option to keep it closed.

There are two versions of this idea, and they behave very differently when only a hash function is available.

Time-lock puzzle

The sender picks a key, encrypts the transaction under it, and attaches a puzzle whose solution is the key. Anyone can solve the puzzle, but it takes computation that cannot be parallelized: at least T seconds on the fastest hardware, and several times longer on ordinary hardware. Each sender attaches its own puzzle, and each puzzle is solved separately.

Delay encryption

Senders encrypt to a public label, such as a slot number (in Ethereum, a slot is the 12-second interval in which one block is proposed). After the slot, one T-second computation on that label yields a key that opens every transaction sent to it. Encryption is cheap and the network solves once per slot.

Time-lock puzzles are known from repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, and newer candidates come from lattices. Delay encryption is known from isogenies. The group and isogeny constructions are broken by quantum computers, and the RSA and isogeny ones need a trusted setup. This note asks what a hash function alone can do. Throughout, the hash is SHA-256.

Two observations make a hash-only time lock possible at all. First, the secrecy only needs to be short-lived. The classical barriers say hashes cannot give public-key encryption that stays secure forever against every efficient attacker, but a time lock only has to hold for a minute against an attacker with a fixed hardware budget, and it is meant to open afterwards. Second, the sender and the opener hash in different ways: the sender can hash in parallel, while the opener must hash in sequence. The construction turns the sender's parallel width into the opener's delay.

Summary of results

  1. Time-lock puzzles work. A set of hash chains linked as in section 2 gives a per-transaction time lock, with no trusted setup and no committee; the table above gives its costs. Prior work proves the idealized puzzle secure against quantum attackers (section 7); our concrete bound is classical.
  2. The delay is proved, given one hardware number. In the random-oracle model, a classical attacker derives the key early only by guessing a 24-byte link or matching a 32-byte value: against the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network, the bound is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock and 2−97 for a one-year lock (section 3). Turning oracle rounds into seconds needs ℓ, the fastest time for one chained SHA-256. We take ℓ = 3.5 ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published core we found; if ℓ is off by a factor k, the delay shrinks by k and nothing else breaks, and section 5 shows the cost of every choice.
  3. The sender's cost cannot be avoided, but it can be paid early. For constructions from a random oracle, the sender's total work is asymptotically optimal (section 7). It spends about 0.70 seconds of laptop CPU time per second of delay, or 0.097 on an M4 Max GPU amortized over a batch that takes hours, and puzzles can be built in advance (section 5).
  4. Delay encryption is out of reach. Encrypting to a slot so that one computation opens all its transactions is impossible with hashes alone when the setup is cheap. With an expensive setup the question is open. No known method opens puzzles from different senders for less than one full solve each.
  5. It works as a timed commitment. Behind commit–reveal, users normally reveal their keys after ordering, and nobody solves anything. For a well-formed puzzle, a silent user's transaction can still be forced open, in about 6.7 minutes on one laptop core, so withholding the key delays it but cannot cancel it. A deployment must handle malformed puzzles separately and make its penalties enforceable (section 6).
  6. An implementation exists. chainseal is a complete, specified format with a Rust implementation, a second implementation in Python, test vectors and benchmarks (section 4).

What is new. Neither the construction nor its practical use is new: Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan gave the chained puzzle, and several tools implement it (section 7). What this note adds is a concrete security proof for chainseal's exact encoding together with its sensitivity to the hardware numbers (section 3), parameters for sealing transactions with costs measured on current CPUs and GPUs (sections 4 and 5), a specified and tested implementation (section 4), and its use as a fallback for commit–reveal (section 6).

2. The construction

A hash chain starts from a value and hashes it N times in a row. Each step needs the previous output, so if the hash has no shortcut and its outputs cannot be guessed, even unlimited hardware needs N steps one after another. A single chain would make a fine lock, except that its builder also needs N sequential steps: a one-minute lock would take the sender a minute on the fastest hardware there is, and much longer on a phone. The construction fixes this by building many short chains at the same time and linking them in a fixed order.

The sender builds n chains of m hashes each, all at once, and links them so that an opener can only run them in order. Its parameters:

nnumber of chains16
mhashes per chain230 ≈ 1.1 billion

N = n·mtotal hashes, which sets the delay234 ≈ 17 billion

We call n = 16, m = 230, b = 24 the reference parameters. They give a one-minute delay against an attacker at 3.5 ns per hash, with a 452-byte puzzle. Sections 3 and 5 explain the choice: the delay fixes N; 24-byte links keep the proved bound near 2−96 or below for delays up to a year; and n only trades puzzle size against how many cores one puzzle can use.

Building and opening the puzzle

Figure 1 gives the two algorithms. They follow sections 4 and 5 of SPEC.md exactly, with the byte encodings left out.

The sender's work is in lines 3 to 5 of Algorithm 1: n chains of m steps, each on its own core. Every chain step hashes the 32-byte state with the salt and the chain's index. That is still one SHA-256 compression, and it makes each step specific to one chain of one puzzle. The rest is a few hashes per chain. Each link after the first is published only masked, under a hash of the end of the chain before it (line 7). The key is a hash of all the chain ends in order, taken one at a time so that an opener carries a single 32-byte value from chain to chain (lines 8 to 10). The commitment C lets anyone check a claimed key with one hash. The sender then encrypts the transaction under K with authenticated encryption (section 4) and keeps the puzzle and key until it needs them.

An opener without the key has only x1. It runs chain 1, uses its end to unhide x2, runs chain 2, and so on; Figure 2 shows the two schedules side by side. The only way to start a chain early is to guess its link, which the security theorem bounds (section 3).

Why it works

  • Opening is sequential. Chain i + 1 cannot start until its link is known, and that link is hidden by the end of chain i. So opening takes N = n·m hashes one after another, however much hardware the opener has. Section 3 proves this in the random-oracle model.
  • Building is parallel. The sender knows every link from the start, so it runs all n chains at once. With a core for every chain its wall time is m steps; in general it is about N divided by its total hash rate. The few extra hashes per chain, to start it and hide its link, and the two at the end are negligible.
  • Size and delay are separate. The puzzle consists of the n links, n·b bytes, plus 68 bytes for the parameters, the salt and the commitment. The delay depends on N, so longer chains give a longer delay with the same puzzle size.

Four design choices

  • Every hash names its puzzle and its chain. Each chain step hashes the state together with the salt and the chain's index, and every other hash includes the parameters and the salt. Work done on another puzzle, or before this one existed, does not help.
  • Masks are hashes of chain ends. zi hides xi+1 under a hash of yi, not under yi itself. So learning a link, by guessing it or otherwise, says nothing about the end of the chain before it.
  • The key depends on every end. An opener that somehow learns a later link must still finish every chain, and it cannot check a guessed link against C until its own chains reach that link. Had the key been derived from the last end alone, a right guess of xn could be checked after one chain (section 3).
  • Orderings bind the whole sealed transaction. Once K is known, anyone can seal a different message under the same puzzle with a valid tag. A system that orders sealed transactions must therefore commit to the hash of the whole sealed transaction, not to the puzzle or to C (section 6).

Worked example: the reference parameters

n = 16 chains of m = 230 hashes with 24-byte links: 234 hashes in total and a 452-byte puzzle. An attacker that computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns needs 60 seconds to open it. Measured on an M4 MacBook Air: building took 42.1 seconds on its CPU, and opening took 6 min 42 s on one core. The opened key matched the commitment and decrypted the transaction.

3. Security

Secrecy until the deadline rests on two assumptions about SHA-256 and two about the attacker's hardware. This section states them, proves the delay in the random-oracle model, explains how we price the one hardware number that the proof cannot supply, and says what happens if any of the numbers is wrong. It then goes through the attacks one by one and ends with the choice of link width.

The delay theorem

Model. The chain steps F1, …, Fn and the labelled hashes of Figure 1 are independent random oracles. The attacker is a classical randomized algorithm A = (A0, A1) with unbounded computation. A0 runs before the puzzle exists and makes Qpre queries; its whole state passes to A1, which receives the puzzle and makes Qon queries. Let Q = Qpre + Qon. Time after publication is counted in oracle rounds: all queries in a round are chosen before any answer from that round arrives. More hardware allows more queries in a round, but no query can use the answer of another query in the same round.

Honest schedule. Computing each chain's mask and accumulator in the same round, Open derives K in oracle round Tkey = n(m + 2) + 1 and returns it, checked against C, one round later. Build takes m + n + 3 rounds: one for the starts, m for the chains, n for the accumulator, with the masks alongside, and one each for K and C. Let P = n(m + 3) + 1, the number of oracle answers Open uses, C included.

Theorem 1 (classical attackers, random-oracle model)

Let (Z, K) ← Build(n, m, b), with randomness independent of the oracles. For every attacker A = (A0, A1) as above,

Pr[ A1 outputs K within fewer than Tkey oracle rounds ]
≤ Qon / 28b + Qpre / 2128+8b + (QP + P2 + 1) / 2256,

and the first term is absent when n = 1.

What it means in seconds. Assumptions A2 and A3 turn oracle rounds and queries into time. Write T = n · m · ℓ, and let the attacker make Qon = R · T queries after publication. Against R = 1021 hashes per second, roughly the whole Bitcoin network, the bound is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock and 2−97 for a one-year lock. Precomputation barely changes this: a full year of queries at the same rate before a one-minute puzzle is published adds about 2−127. With 24-byte links the first term, the chance of guessing a link, dominates; for a one-minute lock the full-width terms are smaller by a factor of about 230. At a fixed delay the bound depends on the number of chains only weakly, through P ≈ N, except that the first term is absent when n = 1. It covers every saving, however small: except with this probability, the attacker does not derive the key even one oracle round before the honest opener.

Scope. The threshold is the round in which K is derived, not the round in which Open returns: an attacker that skips the check against C finishes one round before Open does, with certainty. The precomputation term is needed even when n = 1, where the only link is public: an attacker that guesses the salt and the link in advance and computes the key opens the puzzle in zero rounds with probability 2−(128+8b). The theorem is about recovering the key. The encryption layer is intended to hide the contents of equal-length messages under A1 and A4, but a reduction from distinguishing envelopes to the events of the proof, which must also count queries on the derivation of the encryption and authentication keys, has not been written. The bound is classical; extending it to this instantiation against quantum attackers is outside the scope of this note.

Proof. We compare the real experiment with an ideal one in which the values Open computes are drawn only when the attacker reaches them, show that the ideal experiment is sequential, and bound the probability that the two differ.

Path values. For each chain i, let yi,0 = si = Hstart(ctx, i, xi) and yi,k = Fi(yi,k−1) for k = 1, …, m, so that yi,m = yi. Let μi = Hmask(ctx, yi) for i < n, a0 = ctx, ai = Hacc(ai−1, yi), K = Hkey(ctx, an) and C = Hcommit(ctx, K). These P values are the path values. Each path value v is the answer on one path input u(v). The predecessors of v are the path values in u(v), at most two; in addition, μi−1 is a predecessor of si for i ≥ 2, since xi = zi−1 ⊕ [μi−1]b. Let ρ(v) be the oracle round in which Open receives v: ρ(si) = (i − 1)(m + 2) + 1, ρ(yi,k) = ρ(si) + k, ρ(μi) = ρ(ai) = ρ(yi) + 1 and ρ(K) = Tkey. Every path value v other than s1 satisfies ρ(v) = 1 + max ρ(w) over its predecessors w.

The two experiments. In the real experiment R, the oracles are random functions sampled lazily; A0 runs, Build runs, and A1 receives Z. The ideal experiment G uses the same lazily sampled oracles, but Build makes no queries. It draws the salt and x1 as before, and draws z1, …, zn−1 and C uniformly. A query of A1 is legitimate if, when it is submitted, it equals u(v) for a path value v whose predecessors have all been revealed; for si with i ≥ 2 this defines xi = zi−1 ⊕ [μi−1]b. G answers a legitimate query with v, drawing v uniformly the first time (C was drawn already), and marks v revealed. It answers every other query, and all queries of A0, from the lazily sampled oracles. When A1 stops, G draws the path values never revealed, which fixes the remaining links.

Claim 1. In G, no path value v is revealed before oracle round ρ(v). By induction on ρ(v); for s1, with ρ = 1, there is nothing to show. A value is revealed only by a legitimate query, which is submitted after all its predecessors are revealed. Answers arrive at the end of a round, so if a predecessor w is revealed in round ρ(w) or later, v is revealed in round ρ(w) + 1 or later; hence in round 1 + max ρ(w) = ρ(v) or later.

Claim 2. Let Bad be the event, in G, that (a) two path values are equal, or (b) a query that is not legitimate equals a path input, evaluated with the path values as finally drawn. Then the probability that A1 outputs K early in R is at most that in G plus PrG[Bad]. Run both experiments on the same randomness, with the oracle's answers on path inputs in R taken to be the path values drawn in G. If (a) fails, the path inputs are distinct, so these answers are independent and uniform as in R; the puzzle has the same distribution in both, because zi = [μi]b ⊕ xi+1 is uniform when xi+1 is, and C is an answer on a fresh input. If (b) fails, every query either is legitimate, and receives the path value in both experiments, or is not a path input, and receives the same oracle answer in both. So the two transcripts agree unless Bad happens.

Claim 3. PrG[Bad] ≤ Qon / 28b + Qpre / 2128+8b + (QP + P2) / 2256. In G, a path value that is not revealed when a query is submitted is drawn later, independently of that query. So is xi for i ≥ 2 while μi−1 is not revealed, and so are the salt and x1 for every query of A0. Nothing is conditioned on: each bound below is the probability that a fixed query equals a value drawn after it.

  • (a): at most P2 pairs of independent uniform 32-byte values, so probability at most P2 / 2256.
  • (b), queries to Hstart: a query names one index i and equals u(si) only if it contains xi. A query of A1 with i = 1 is legitimate whenever it equals u(s1). One with i ≥ 2 that is not legitimate was submitted while μi−1 was not revealed, so it contains xi with probability 2−8b; this case needs n ≥ 2. A query of A0 must also contain the salt, so it succeeds with probability 2−(128+8b).
  • (b), all other queries: a query that is not legitimate but equals u(v) contains, in a 32-byte field, a predecessor of v that was not revealed when it was submitted. That happens with probability at most 2−256 for each of the at most P path inputs, so at most P / 2256 per query.

Summing over the Qon and Qpre queries gives the claim.

Conclusion. In G, if A1 stops before round Tkey, then K is not revealed by Claim 1, so it is drawn after A1's output, and the output equals it with probability 2−256; C, drawn independently, tells A nothing about it. Claims 2 and 3 add PrG[Bad]. ∎

The proof follows the structure of Pietrzak and Reyzin's soundness proof for proofs of sequential work (their Lemmas 6 and 9). SPEC.md section 8 gives the same argument for chainseal's byte encoding. It has not been machine-checked or reviewed by a third party.

The attacker's total hash rate R is easier to price, because the hardware exists. A Bitcoin miner (S21 XP) computes 270 TH/s for about $2.8k, an RTX 5090 GPU 27 GH/s (hashcat), and the Bitcoin network about 1021 H/s (October 2026). A mining pipeline finishes one double SHA-256 per clock cycle, every 3 ns in a 2013 chip at about 335 MHz (Taylor 2013), but each hash spends many cycles in the pipeline. These machines compute many independent hashes at once and say little about how fast one hash can follow another, so they bound R in A3, not ℓ; we take R = 1021 H/s.

Attacks

  • Guess a link. An attacker can guess a hidden link xk and run chains k, k + 1, … from the guess while its own chains 1 to k − 1 are still running. This is the attack behind the first term of the theorem. With 24-byte links each guess is right with probability 2−192. Two choices of section 2 also limit what a right guess would buy, which would matter only for shorter links (Appendix A). Because the key needs the end of every chain, a guess cannot be checked against the commitment C until the attacker's own chains reach link k; had the key been derived from the last chain's end alone, a guess of xn could be checked after one chain, and a right guess would open the puzzle after T / n. Because the mask is a hash of the previous end rather than the end itself, a right guess of a link reveals nothing about the chain before it.
  • Guess the key. A guess of the 32-byte key is checked against C with one hash and is right with chance 2−256.
  • Precompute. Every puzzle draws a fresh random salt, and every hash on the honest path depends on it together with a fresh link or a 32-byte value computed from one. So work done before a puzzle is published, or on another puzzle, is of no use, except with the negligible probability the theorem already counts. Once a puzzle is public, an attacker could compute chain segments for it from states of its choice, hoping the honest chain passes through one. That is the 3QP / 2256 term.
  • Use a quantum computer. Afshar et al. (2023) prove that the idealized chained puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan keeps its sequential delay against quantum openers in the quantum random-oracle model. Carrying that result over to chainseal's exact encoding, with its truncated links, start values, accumulator, commitment and envelope, is a separate argument that has not been made. Our concrete bound is classical.
  • Get the puzzle early. The attacker's clock starts when the puzzle is published. A sender who builds puzzles in advance must keep them private until use.
  • Lie about the key, or replace the message. A claimed key is checked against C with one hash, and authenticated decryption then checks the transaction, so a wrong key is rejected at once. Once the key is known, though, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle, so the ordering must bind the whole sealed transaction (section 6). Nothing checks in advance that a puzzle is well formed; see malformed puzzles in section 6.

With n = 16 the format's limit m ≤ 248 caps the delay at about 182 days at 3.5 ns, so a one-year lock needs at least 33 chains and a larger puzzle; at a fixed delay the bound depends on n only weakly, through P, except that its first term vanishes when n = 1.

We use 24-byte links, which keep the bound at about 2−96 or below for delays up to one year under the stated attacker budget. In general, to make the guessing term at most 2−λ, choose b ≥ ⌈(log2 Q + λ) / 8⌉ and then check that the full-width terms are also small; for λ = 96 and a year at 1021 H/s, log2 Q ≈ 94.7 and the rule gives 24. Twenty-byte links would give about 2−84 for a minute and 2−65 for a year. With 32-byte links the guessing term falls below the other terms, so the link width drops out of the bound. That adds about 30 bits of margin for a one-minute lock and 11 for a one-year lock, at a cost of 128 bytes per puzzle, 17% of a sealed transfer. It does not protect against underestimating the attacker: both terms grow in proportion to Q, so an attacker k times stronger than assumed costs the same log2 k bits with either width. This note relies only on the theorem. Appendix A discusses a sharper bound for 16-byte links, which we do not use.

4. Implementation and measurements

chainseal implements section 2 as a complete, versioned format. A sender builds puzzles in advance and, when a transaction is ready, encrypts it under one. Anyone can then open the result in one of two ways: with the key, if the sender reveals it, or by solving the puzzle. A solve saves its progress every 10 seconds, so it can be stopped and resumed, and several puzzles can be solved at once, one per core. The code is about 4,000 lines of Rust, whose only dependencies are getrandom for the operating system's random numbers and signal-hook for stopping a solve cleanly. It has been run on macOS on Apple M4 and M4 Max laptops and on Linux on an AMD EPYC 9554P, and continuous integration builds and tests it on Linux for x86-64 and AArch64 and on macOS. A separate program of about 560 lines measures the chain step on Apple GPUs with Metal.

Encryption uses only SHA-256. The key gives an HMAC-SHA256 keystream, and a 32-byte HMAC tag covers the whole sealed transaction, so whoever lacks the key cannot change the puzzle or the ciphertext without the change being detected when the transaction is opened.   • A written specification fixes every byte of every format, with test vectors: 7 valid cases and 51 malformed inputs that an implementation must reject.
  • A second implementation, in Python, shares no code with the Rust one; it uses Python's own SHA-256 and HMAC and its own parsers. The two agree on every test vector, and each opens the other's sealed messages.
  • Tests. Known answers for SHA-256 and HMAC, every SHA-256 backend against plain SHA-256, round trips over many parameter sets, and end-to-end tests of the command-line tool, including interrupting and resuming a solve. A property test feeds the four decoders 480,000 mutated encodings in all, 120,000 each, and cargo-fuzz ran each decoder for two minutes under AddressSanitizer, about 108 million inputs in all; neither found a fault. The only unsafe code is in the two hardware backends.
  • AI-assisted review of the code and the specification by an AI model, GPT-6 Astra. It reviewed the earlier C implementation and the specification over several rounds, each checking the fixes for the previous one. Among its findings were that using a chain end directly as a mask leaked that end, and a bug in resuming a finished solve; both were fixed. Its last round found no blocker or major finding. The reports and the fixes are in the repository. The current Rust implementation replaced the reviewed C one; it reproduces the C version's test vectors and command-line behaviour and was checked against it, but has not had a review of its own.

Limits of this evaluation. No human cryptographer has reviewed the code or the format. Where the building time comes from

A sender spends N / Rs = T / (ℓ · Rs) seconds building a T-second lock, so the cost is best stated as a ratio: 1/(ℓ · Rs) seconds of building per second of delay. It is the product of two factors. The assumed attacker computes one chained hash 6.6 times faster than one of the laptop's cores (3.5 ns against 23.2 ns), and the laptop as a whole hashes 10.6 times faster than one core running one chain (461 M/s against 43.3 M/s); 6.6 / 10.6 ≈ 0.62. The measured ratio, 0.70, is a little higher, because building one puzzle does not keep every core at its best rate to the end. For constructions from a random oracle the number of hashes cannot be reduced asymptotically (section 7), so only these two factors can change: the attacker's assumed advantage, and the sender's total hash rate. Neither needs to change much for the scheme to be usable, because the building time is not spent while the transaction waits.

  • Build in advance. A puzzle does not depend on the transaction it will seal, so a wallet can keep a stock of puzzles and build more while the machine is idle or charging. The building time is then a cost per transaction, not a delay. On the laptop's CPU, all cores running for a day would build about 2,100 one-minute puzzles; most users send far fewer transactions.
  • Build in bulk on a GPU. A GPU has no SHA-256 instructions, but it has many more lanes. On the laptop, Metal computes 0.71 billion chained hashes per second, 1.5 times the CPU, which brings the ratio to 0.4 and the cost of a one-minute puzzle to 24 s. On the M4 Max, the GPU computes 2.96 billion per second, 3.4 times its CPU, or 5.8 s per puzzle. 6. Using it in Ethereum

This section sketches how chainseal could back commit–reveal in Ethereum and lists what such a protocol would still have to specify. Keeping a transaction's contents hidden until its place in the order is irrevocably fixed prevents front-running based on those contents. It does not by itself give the other properties of an encrypted mempool, such as fair inclusion or hiding the sender, the fee and the size.

  • Commit–reveal with a fallback. This is a timed commitment. The protocol commits to a hash of the complete sealed transaction. The puzzle's commitment C binds its decryption key; revealing the key, or recovering it from a well-formed puzzle, opens the committed transaction. The user sends the encrypted transaction, the puzzle and the commitment C. After ordering, the user reveals K, and nodes check H(K) = C. The ordering must commit to the whole sealed transaction, not just to C: once K is known, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle. Solving is needed only for users who stay silent, and a deposit can charge them for it under a rule the protocol can observe. Execution rules must say what a transaction opened late may still do.
  • Opening silent users' puzzles needs dedicated hardware. To open within about T, solvers need chips as fast as the attacker the delay is priced against. On a laptop, a one-minute puzzle takes about 6.7 minutes. Either the network runs solver chips, or late-opened transactions execute in a later slot.
  • Solving cost. If every transaction in a 12-second slot, about 265 in the blocks we sampled, had to be opened by solvers, with one-minute puzzles, that would keep about 1,300 attacker-speed chain circuits busy at all times, or about 8,800 M4 cores. In the normal case almost none are needed, since users reveal their keys.
  • Bandwidth. Sealing adds 128 + n · b bytes to a transaction, 512 bytes with the reference parameters: the 452-byte puzzle and 60 bytes for the encryption. A signed ETH transfer is about 110 bytes, so sealed it takes 622 bytes, about 5.7 times more. Most transactions are larger than a transfer: in a small, reproducible snapshot of 20 consecutive mainnet blocks (26,249,774 to 26,249,793, 22 October 2026; 5,300 transactions), the median transaction was 179 bytes and the mean about 790, so sealing makes the median transaction about 3.9 times larger and the mean about 1.6 times. The sample can be reproduced with bench/eth-sample.py, and its data is in bench-results/eth-sample.json. Twenty blocks support these observations, not long-term averages.
  • Gas and block space. Sent as calldata, the added bytes are nearly all random, so their price depends on which calldata rule applies. We give three scenarios for the 512 bytes. At the standard price of 16 gas per nonzero byte they cost about 8,200 gas, against 21,000 for a plain transfer. A sealed transaction that does little computation falls under the calldata floor of EIP-7623, 40 gas per byte, or about 20,500 gas. EIP-7976, proposed for the Glamsterdam upgrade, would raise the floor to 64 gas per byte, about 33,000 gas. At the sample's base fee of about 0.2 gwei and an ETH price of $2,400, even the last costs under 2 cents. A contract should not store the puzzle: storage costs over 20,000 gas per 32-byte word, about 320,000 gas for 512 bytes. It needs to keep only the 32-byte hash of the sealed transaction, which binds the ordering to it, and the puzzle only has to stay available until it is opened. The sampled blocks held about 265 transactions and used 29 million gas each, so the framing alone, 512 bytes per transaction, would add about 2.2 million gas per block at the standard price, or about 5.4 million under the EIP-7623 floor. That is framing overhead only. Encryption also turns existing calldata, 60% of whose bytes are zero in the sample, into nearly random bytes; at the standard price that adds about 1.2 million gas more, for about 3.4 million, 12% of the sampled blocks' gas. A total for a protocol also depends on its transaction encoding, the reveal and the execution path, which are not specified here. A blob carries about 127 KB at a separate and usually much lower price, enough for about 250 puzzles, and is kept for about 18 days, far longer than any puzzle needs.
  • Energy and phones. One puzzle took 42.1 s on the laptop's CPU at an estimated 20 W, about 0.23 Wh. That would be about 1% of a 20 Wh phone battery if a phone were as efficient; no phone was measured. Current phones have 6 to 8 CPU cores, most of them efficiency cores, and all have SHA-256 instructions. A wallet can build its stock of puzzles while charging (section 5), or outsource building to several services (section 7).
  • Malformed puzzles. As with any time-lock puzzle, the forced opening works only if the puzzle is well formed. A sender could build a puzzle whose key matches C but whose chains lead elsewhere, reveal K if it likes the ordering, and otherwise stay silent and let the solve fail. A deposit forfeited when a solve fails makes this costly, and it also pays for the wasted solve, but only if the protocol can verify the failure: a missed reveal is directly observable, whereas a claim that a long solve ended in a commitment mismatch is not cheap to check. A trusted builder could instead check puzzles. PVDE removes the problem for RSA puzzles with a zero-knowledge proof; for chained hashes, a proof must be far cheaper to check than the chains themselves (section 8). Fees and deposits must be collectable without the key, and the number of open puzzles per sender should be capped.
  • Related designs. Threshold-encrypted mempools release keys from a committee: Shutter's keypers, Ferveo (Bebel and Ojha 2022) and FairBlock (Momeni, Gorbunov and Zhang 2022), with batched schemes that keep unselected transactions private (Choudhuri et al. 2024); Passerat-Palmbach (2026) surveys them. Radius's PVDE (2022) seals each transaction under an RSA time-lock puzzle with a zero-knowledge proof that the puzzle is well formed, and multiparty delay encryption (Khajehpour et al. 2023) opens a whole batch with one timed computation. Buterin proposed time-locked transactions as an alternative to commit–reveal in 2018, and later contrasted costly per-transaction time locks with delay encryption (2023, 2024). Voluntary opening backed by forced opening is the pattern of timed commitments (Boneh and Naor 2000). chainseal plays the role of PVDE's puzzle with a hash in place of RSA: it needs no setup and no committee, at the price of more work for the sender and a puzzle that cannot be checked in advance.

When the delay is enough

Two clocks matter for one sealed transaction. The attacker's clock starts at exposure, texp, the first moment anyone but the sender holds the puzzle: when the transaction is broadcast, or earlier if the puzzle leaked. The application's deadline, torder, is the moment the transaction's place in the order can no longer change. The contents stay hidden until the deadline if

torder − texp < n · m · ℓattacker,

with a margin for propagation and for whatever the ordering rule itself assumes. Forced recovery is a separate matter: it finishes at tsolve + n · m · ℓsolver, where tsolve is when a solver starts, which on a laptop is minutes after the deadline. For the puzzle, an attacker k times faster than assumed shortens the delay k-fold, a gradual loss. For the application the loss is sudden: once n · m · ℓattacker falls below torder − texp, the contents can be read before the order is fixed.

7. Related work, and what hashes cannot do

This section places chainseal among earlier work: why Merkle's hash-based key exchange does not give a time lock, the theory and implementations of chained puzzles, and what hash functions provably cannot do.

Merkle puzzles, and why they do not help

The classical hash-based way to keep a secret from an eavesdropper is Merkle's 1974 puzzles. Alice publishes k puzzles, each breakable with about k work. Bob breaks one at random and announces its identifier, and the two share its secret. The eavesdropper does not know which puzzle Bob chose, so she must break about half of them, about k2 work in total.

Impagliazzo and Rudich (1989) showed that key exchange secure against every efficient eavesdropper cannot be built from a random oracle in a black-box way, and Barak and Mahmoody (2009) that in the random-oracle model Merkle's quadratic gap is the best possible. For a time lock, though, a gap in total work is worth nothing: the eavesdropper's k2 is k separate pieces of work, each as large as Bob's, so on k machines it takes Bob's time. An attacker who can rent hardware and only needs to win a one-minute race does exactly that. What helps is a gap in sequential time, which a chain provides.

Prior work

Theory. Rivest, Shamir and Wagner (1996) introduced time-lock puzzles, based on repeated squaring in an RSA group; Bitansky et al. (2016) built them from randomized encodings. Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) studied time-lock puzzles in the random-oracle model, which treats the hash as a random function anyone can query. They gave the chained puzzle of section 2, including a variant that iterates the hash in each link so that the puzzle stays small for a long delay (their section 3.1). They also proved that no puzzle in this model can force more sequential rounds than the sender's total number of hash calls, up to a constant factor. Two neighbouring notions need different things. A proof of sequential work shows that time has passed, but its creator knows no secret answer in advance (Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan 2013; Cohen and Pietrzak 2018). A verifiable delay function needs a unique output that anyone can check quickly (Boneh et al. 2018), and that is impossible from a random oracle alone (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2025; Abusalah et al. 2026). Concrete bounds exist for computing a full-width hash chain with parallel queries, classically and against quantum attackers (Chung et al. 2021; Blocki, Lee and Zhou 2021). They cover a single chain, not several chains linked through masked secrets as in chainseal.

Practice. The chained-hash idea has been implemented several times. Gwern Branwen's 2011 essay described it. Dorian Johnson's Go tool (2013) builds SHA-512 chains in parallel, with full 64-byte seeds, each encrypted under the end of the previous chain with AES. Peter Todd's timelock (2014) uses SHA-256 chains, and Todd published a 32-chain puzzle with Bitcoin bounties on its intermediate secrets. Kadupul (Skjegstad, Madhavapeddy and Crowcroft 2014) uses Todd's puzzles to reward packet forwarding. EtherDream's Timelock Online (2024) builds chains in a browser on CPUs and GPUs, with 4-byte seeds and a salt per seed. Its slow hash is PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256, and, as in chainseal, its key depends on the end of every chain (section 3). Separately, Solana's Proof of History uses sequential SHA-256 as a clock rather than for encryption (Yakovenko 2018); Shoup (2022) discusses how its elapsed time depends on hardware. None of these states a concrete security bound or measures costs against an explicit attacker model.

What hashes can and cannot do

The impossibility results below are proved in the random-oracle model: the hash is a random function that anyone can call, many times in parallel, and delay is counted in rounds of calls. Turning rounds into seconds takes the hardware assumption of section 3. Items marked as not proved or not analyzed are not theorems.

Possible with hashes alone
  • A per-transaction time lock with any delay, costing the sender work in proportion to the delay.
  • A puzzle whose size does not depend on the delay: about n · b bytes.
  • Security against known quantum attacks (proved for the idealized puzzle, not for this instantiation).
  • One puzzle covering many transactions from the same sender.
  • Building outsourced to k services: each builds a puzzle and returns it with its key, and the sender encrypts under a hash of all k keys. The key stays secret if any one service is honest, the delay is unchanged, and size and opening work grow k-fold. Any one service can still spoil the result by sending a bad puzzle. Not analyzed formally.
  • Commit–reveal in which withholding the key delays a transaction with a well-formed puzzle but cannot cancel it.
  • Open: whether a puzzle smaller than about g · b bytes exists for a sender working g ways in parallel (section 8).
Not possible with hashes alone
  • A provable delay longer than the sender's total hashing, run one hash after another.
  • Delay encryption: senders encrypt cheaply to a slot, and one computation opens all of it (proved when the setup is cheap).

The delay is limited by the sender's work

Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) proved the following. If building a puzzle takes N hash calls in total, an attacker can open it in about N rounds, where in each round it may compute any number of hashes in parallel. The intuition: whatever makes the puzzle solvable is hidden among the N hashes the sender computed. In each round the attacker guesses a complete run of the sender that fits the puzzle and everything learned so far, and computes all of that run's hashes at once. Each round either opens the puzzle or uncovers at least one more hash the real sender used, and there are only N of those.

This attack is expensive. Each round makes a huge number of hash calls, and the version with N rounds also needs unlimited computation between rounds; an efficient version needs about N/ε rounds to succeed except with probability ε. So the result is a limit on what any proof can promise, not a cheap way to open puzzles. The chained puzzle needs about N rounds, so it is asymptotically optimal in the random-oracle model: its proved delay grows linearly with the sender's work, and no hash-only puzzle can do better than linear.

In practice: no proof can promise that a hash-only puzzle keeps an attacker busy for much longer than the sender's total number of hashes, run one after another at the attacker's speed. For chainseal, a one-minute lock against a 3.5 ns attacker takes about 234 chain steps, spread over as many cores as the sender likes. The result is asymptotic, with unspecified constants and an attacker of unlimited total work, so it does not give an exact lower bound on the sender's cost for every construction under the fixed attacker budget used here.

Delay encryption is out of reach

Suppose a public setup costs S hash calls, after which senders can encrypt to slot 1234 with E hash calls, while computing the slot's key takes T seconds of sequential work. Then running the setup and encrypting a random message is itself a time-lock puzzle that costs S + E hash calls to build. By the result above, its message can be recovered in about S + E rounds. If the setup is cheap, the delay is limited by the cost of one encryption, which is the opposite of what delay encryption promises.

This argument does not settle the case of a long setup that itself uses the hash: then S + E is large and the bound says nothing useful. If, in addition, each slot has only one key that passes a quick public check, computing it is a verifiable delay function, and those are impossible from hashes alone even with an expensive private setup (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2025; Abusalah et al. 2026).

Batching and puzzle size

Independent puzzles share nothing, so solving one teaches nothing about another, and no method is known that opens several for less than a full sequential solve each. A sender can still put many transactions under one key and one puzzle. The version that would help a mempool, where unrelated senders encrypt privately to a common slot without knowing its key, is delay encryption, covered above.

On size: each chain the sender runs in parallel needs its own hidden link of about b bytes, so a chained puzzle that lets the sender work g ways in parallel has about g · b bytes. Whether some cleverer hash-only puzzle can be smaller is open. Lower bounds of this kind on communication are known only for restricted kinds of key exchange (Haitner et al. 2019), not for time-lock puzzles.

8. Open questions

For per-transaction time locks, no new construction is needed: the chained puzzle is asymptotically optimal among random-oracle constructions, its delay is proved in the random-oracle model (though the proof has not been checked by machine or by independent reviewers), the parameters are workable, and an implementation exists. What is missing is a better estimate of the attacker's hardware, a cheap way to check that a puzzle is well formed, measurements on more hardware, and the network design. For delay encryption, new ideas are needed, and they must go beyond hashes, at least when the setup is cheap. The open work, roughly in order of importance:

  1. How fast can chained SHA-256 be? Every parameter is priced against this. A careful circuit-level estimate, ideally from chip designers, would turn the advantage factor A ≈ 3.7, that is 3.5 ns, into a defensible number. An alternative is a hash whose sequential speed differs less between laptops and custom chips.
  2. Checking puzzles before solving. A deposit makes a malformed puzzle costly; a check would rule it out (section 6). A proof over all n · m chain steps would work, but with current proof systems it would cost far more than building the puzzle. Is there a cheaper check, for example one that spot-checks the chains or the links between them?
  3. A sharper bound for short links. A reviewed proof of the bound in Appendix A would bound the chance of saving a whole chain more tightly for short links. Whether that justifies 16-byte links depends on what saving and what probability a deployment tolerates: savings of less than a chain would still be bounded only by the theorem, about 2−52 for a one-minute lock, short of the 96-bit target used here.
  4. Measurements on more hardware, and building on GPUs. chainseal has been timed on two Apple laptops and one x86 server core (37 ns per chained hash), and the chain step on two Apple GPUs. Timing it on x86 desktops, phones and discrete GPUs would replace the remaining estimates in section 5, and the tool should build batches of puzzles on a GPU itself.
  5. A lower bound on puzzle size. Chained puzzles need about b bytes per chain the sender runs in parallel. Does every hash-only time lock that lets the sender work g ways in parallel need size proportional to g, or is there a smaller puzzle?
  6. Mechanism design. Who runs solvers and how they are paid (a published key can be copied, so the reward cannot simply go to whoever posts it first), who pays for silent users, how late-opened transactions execute, how malformed puzzles are handled, and how the puzzle bytes fit the bandwidth budget.
  7. Delay encryption beyond hashes. Compare isogeny, lattice and batch-solvable constructions on setup, quantum security and cost against the hash-only baseline measured here.

Appendix A. A sharper bound for short links

Nothing in the main text depends on this appendix. It records what we know about short links, in case a smaller puzzle is worth the extra assumption.

The theorem of section 3 charges the attacker as if one right guess of a link could save all the remaining work, so its guessing term, Q / 28b, does not depend on how much is saved. That is probably loose. Because the key needs the end of every chain, a guessed link is confirmed only when the attacker's own chains reach it, and until then every run from a guess is blind. So a guess that saves a whole chain has itself cost a whole chain of hashes. This suggests:

Conjecture (random-oracle model)

An attacker that makes at most Q queries saves at least d rounds against the honest opener with probability at most about 2Q / (28b · min(d, m)), plus terms of order QP / 2256.

For 16-byte links, a one-minute lock with n = 16 and R = 1021 H/s, this is about 2−81 for saving a whole chain or more. It does not help small savings: for saving one round it is about 2−51, no better than the theorem. The cap at one chain cannot be removed. The security audit (section 4) built attackers against 1- and 2-byte links that defeat two stronger versions: charging each guess its whole saving fails against an attacker that merges guessed runs reaching the same link, and squaring the probability for savings of more than half fails against one that prepares short starts for every value of a link.

Status. An AI model, GPT-6 Astra, wrote a proof of the conjecture with the constant 2, in the model of the theorem without preprocessing. A second model, Claude Opus, checked it step by step and against the known attacks, and found gaps in rigor that it judged fixable but no error. A separate search for counterexamples at toy sizes found none. No person has reviewed the proof, so we do not rely on it. If it holds, it bounds the chance of saving a whole chain with 16-byte links at about 2−81 for a one-minute lock, but savings of less than a chain would still be bounded only by the theorem, so 16-byte links would not meet the 96-bit target used in the main text.

Appendix B. Pricing the hardware number

This appendix gives the reasoning behind assumption A2 and the advantage factor A of section 3.

A2 can be checked against how SHA-256 is computed. One SHA-256 compression is 64 rounds, and each round needs the exact output of the previous one. The new state words are sums of up to five 32-bit terms. Some of them, such as the round constant and the message word, can be added ahead of time, but the next round applies rotations and bitwise functions to the exact bits of the result. So every round must finish at least one 32-bit addition with full carry propagation, plus a few levels of logic. Lee, Chan and Verbauwhede show that this much is also enough: with carry-save adders and retiming, the loop through one round needs one carry-propagate addition, one carry-save layer and the choice function Ch. Pipelining raises throughput but not the latency of one chain, since each step starts from the previous step's output. Unrolling removes the registers between rounds, so the relevant floor is the one for logic alone. So ℓ is roughly 64 times the shortest critical path of one round, measured over many unrolled rounds.

The model row of the table in section 3 puts numbers on this. Its unit is the FO4, the delay of an inverter driving four copies of itself. It prices the round loop of Lee et al. at 10–17 FO4: 7–12 for a fast 32-bit adder (Harris and Sutherland) and 3–5 that we assume for the carry-save layer and Ch. Predicted FO4 delays for 3–5 nm processes are 4.3–4.7 ps (Nikolić et al.). Then 64 rounds take 64 × 10 × 4.3 ps = 2.75 ns to 64 × 17 × 4.7 ps = 5.11 ns, and with 0.1–0.3 ns for the final addition and startup, 2.85–5.41 ns. Our allowance of 1 to 1.8 times for wires, cell choice and registers gives 2.9–9.7 ns, that is A ≈ 1.3–4.5. As a cross-check, Intel's mining patent describes a state update 13 logic levels deep (Suresh et al.); if each level cost one FO4, which is optimistic, a hash would take 3.6–3.9 ns. A process shrink alone gives only A ≈ 1.3. At 3.5 ns a round has 55 ps, about 12 FO4, so the design point needs a very good custom datapath and sits near the aggressive end of the model. The model's lower end is a conditional estimate, not a physical bound, and no published design that we found comes close to it.

Work on verifiable delay functions faced the same question and also answered it with a stated factor: Drake proposed Amax = 10 as a conservative parameter for RSA-based VDFs, hoping that public ASICs would bring it to about 5. Here A = 10 means ℓ = 1.3 ns, about 4.5 FO4 per round, outside the model but not ruled out. Section 8 lists a careful estimate as the most important open question.
