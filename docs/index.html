Time-Lock Encryption from Hashes Alone

A chained-hash puzzle for sealing transactions, what it costs, what hashes cannot do, and an implementation

Code, specification and benchmark logs: github.com/quangvdao/chainseal

Abstract

An encrypted mempool lets block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run. Each transaction must then open once it is ordered, without a committee that holds a decryption key and without letting its sender keep it sealed. A time-lock puzzle provides this: the transaction opens by itself after a fixed amount of sequential computation. Efficient time-lock puzzles use repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, which needs a trusted setup or is broken by quantum computers. Puzzles built from a hash function avoid both problems, but the sender must compute as many hashes as the opener, only in parallel, and Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan showed that this cost is unavoidable. We ask whether it is affordable for sealing a transaction.

We present chainseal, a practical instantiation of the chained-hash time-lock puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan with SHA-256. The sender computes many hash chains in parallel and links them so that the opener must compute them in sequence. Against an attacker who computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published design, a one-minute lock costs the sender 234 hashes, which took 36.2 s on an Apple M4 laptop (10.2 s for a 15-second lock), and adds 1.1 to 8.1 KiB to the transaction. Puzzles do not depend on the transaction, so a wallet can build them ahead of time. We then use chainseal as a fallback for commit–reveal in Ethereum. Senders normally reveal their keys right after ordering, so no puzzle is ever solved; a sender who withholds its key can then only delay its transaction, not cancel it, as long as its puzzle is well formed.

1. Introduction

In an encrypted mempool, block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run. (In Ethereum, a slot is the 12-second interval in which one block is proposed.) The hard part is opening the transactions afterwards. A committee can hold a decryption key, but then a majority of the committee must be trusted. The sender can reveal its own key after ordering, but then the sender sees the ordering first and can withhold the key when the ordering does not suit it. A time lock avoids both: the transaction opens by itself once a fixed amount of computation has been done, and nobody can do that computation much faster by buying more machines.

There are two versions of this idea, and they behave very differently when only a hash function is available.

Time-lock puzzle

The sender picks a key, encrypts the transaction under it, and attaches a puzzle whose solution is the key. Anyone can solve the puzzle, but it takes T seconds of computation that cannot be parallelized. Each sender attaches its own puzzle, and each puzzle is solved separately.

Delay encryption

Senders encrypt to a public label, such as a slot number. After the slot, one T-second computation on that label yields a key that opens every transaction sent to it. Encryption is cheap and the network solves once per slot.

Time-lock puzzles are known from repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, and newer candidates come from lattices. Delay encryption is known from isogenies. The group and isogeny constructions are broken by quantum computers, and the RSA and isogeny ones need a trusted setup. This note asks what a hash function alone can do. Throughout, the hash is SHA-256.

Summary of results

  1. Time-lock puzzles work. A chain of hashes linked as in section 3 gives a per-transaction time lock. Sealing a transaction for one minute against a very fast attacker costs the sender 234 SHA-256 hashes, spread over all its cores. That took 36.2 seconds on a laptop, and the transaction grows by 1.1–8.1 KiB. There is no trusted setup and no committee, and known quantum attacks are far too slow to matter.
  2. The cost cannot be improved. The sender must do as much hashing as the attacker, only in parallel rather than in sequence. For constructions from a random oracle this is optimal up to a constant factor: no proof can promise a delay much longer than the sender's total number of hashes, run one after another (section 5).
  3. Delay encryption is out of reach. Encrypting to a slot so that one computation opens all its transactions is impossible with hashes alone when the setup is cheap. With an expensive setup the question is open. No known method opens puzzles from different senders for less than one full solve each.
  4. Security rests on SHA-256 and two hardware numbers: the fastest chained SHA-256, ℓ, and the attacker's total hash rate, R. In the random-oracle model, an opener with fewer than N sequential hashes can recover the key only by guessing links, and we state a bound on its chance of doing so, with a proof sketch (section 4). Turning hashes into seconds needs ℓ. If ℓ is misjudged by a factor k, the delay shrinks by k and nothing else breaks; restoring it costs the sender k times more work. We state ℓ as an advantage factor A over the fastest published SHA-256 core, 13.0 ns per hash, and price at A ≈ 3.7, that is ℓ = 3.5 ns. A circuit model built from published round analyses and adder delays puts a chip designed for chains at A ≈ 1.3–4.5, but no such chip has been built. How large A can be is the least certain number in this paper, so section 4 shows the cost of every choice.
  5. The best use is as a fallback. Behind commit–reveal, users normally reveal their keys after ordering, and puzzles are solved only for those who stay silent. If its puzzle is well formed, a user who dislikes the ordering can then delay its transaction but not cancel it (section 8).
  6. An implementation exists. chainseal is a complete, specified format with a Rust implementation, a second implementation in Python, test vectors and benchmarks (section 6).

Two observations make a hash-only time lock possible at all. First, the secrecy only needs to be short-lived. The classical barriers say hashes cannot give public-key encryption that stays secure forever against every efficient attacker, but a time lock only has to hold for a minute against an attacker with a fixed hardware budget, and it is meant to open afterwards. Second, the sender and the opener hash in different ways: the sender can hash in parallel, while the opener must hash in sequence. The construction turns the sender's parallel width into the opener's delay.

2. Background

Hash chains

A hash chain starts from a value and hashes it m times in a row. Each step needs the previous output, so if the hash has no shortcut and its outputs cannot be guessed, even unlimited hardware needs m steps one after another. A single chain would make a fine lock, except that its builder also needs m sequential steps: a one-minute lock would take the sender a minute on the fastest hardware there is, and much longer on a phone. The construction in section 3 fixes this by building many short chains at the same time and linking them in a fixed order.

Merkle puzzles, and why they do not help

The classical hash-based way to keep a secret from an eavesdropper is Merkle's 1974 puzzles. Alice publishes k puzzles, each breakable with about k work. Bob breaks one at random and announces its identifier, and the two share its secret. The eavesdropper does not know which puzzle Bob chose, so she must break about half of them, about k2 work in total.

Impagliazzo and Rudich (1989) showed that key exchange secure against every efficient eavesdropper cannot be built from a random oracle in a black-box way, and Barak and Mahmoody (2009) that in the random-oracle model Merkle's quadratic gap is the best possible. For a time lock, though, a gap in total work is worth nothing: the eavesdropper's k2 is k separate pieces of work, each as large as Bob's, so on k machines it takes Bob's time. An attacker who can rent hardware and only needs to win a one-minute race does exactly that. What helps is a gap in sequential time, which a chain provides.

Prior work

Theory. Rivest, Shamir and Wagner (1996) introduced time-lock puzzles, based on repeated squaring in an RSA group; Bitansky et al. (2016) built them from randomized encodings. Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) studied time-lock puzzles in the random-oracle model, which treats the hash as a random function anyone can query. They gave the chained puzzle of section 3, including a variant that iterates the hash in each link so that the puzzle stays small for a long delay (their section 3.1). They also proved that no puzzle in this model can force more sequential rounds than the sender's total number of hash calls, up to a constant factor. Two neighbouring notions need different things. A proof of sequential work shows that time has passed, but its creator knows no secret answer in advance (Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan 2013; Cohen and Pietrzak 2018). A verifiable delay function needs a unique output that anyone can check quickly (Boneh et al. 2018), and that is impossible from a random oracle alone (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2025; Abusalah et al. 2026). Concrete bounds exist for computing a full-width hash chain with parallel queries, classically and against quantum attackers (Chung et al. 2021; Blocki, Lee and Zhou 2021). They do not cover the short masked links that chainseal relies on.

Practice. The chained-hash idea has been implemented several times. Gwern Branwen's 2011 essay described it. Dorian Johnson's Go tool (2013) builds SHA-512 chains in parallel, with full 64-byte seeds, each encrypted under the end of the previous chain with AES. Peter Todd's timelock (2014) uses SHA-256 chains, and Todd published a 32-chain puzzle with Bitcoin bounties on its intermediate secrets. Kadupul (Skjegstad, Madhavapeddy and Crowcroft 2014) uses Todd's puzzles to reward packet forwarding. EtherDream's Timelock Online (2024) builds chains in a browser on CPUs and GPUs, with 4-byte seeds and a salt per seed. Its slow hash is PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256, and, as in chainseal, its key depends on the end of every chain (section 4). Separately, Solana's Proof of History uses sequential SHA-256 as a clock rather than for encryption (Yakovenko 2018); Shoup (2022) discusses how its elapsed time depends on hardware. None of these states a security bound for short links or measures costs against an explicit attacker model.

This note. Neither the construction nor its practical use is new. What this note adds is an explicit security claim for short links together with its sensitivity to the hardware numbers (section 4), parameters for sealing transactions with costs measured on current hardware (sections 6 and 7), a specified and tested implementation (section 6), and its use as a fallback for commit–reveal (section 8).

3. The construction

The sender builds n chains of m hashes each, all at once, and links them so that an opener can only run them in order. Its parameters:

nnumber of chainsreference value: 512
mhashes per chain225 ≈ 34 million
bbytes per link, the secret that starts each chain12
N = n·mtotal hashes, which sets the delay234 ≈ 17 billion

We call n = 512, m = 225, b = 16 the reference parameters. They give a one-minute delay against an attacker at 3.5 ns per hash.

Building the puzzle (the sender)

  1. Pick a random 16-byte salt and n random links x1, …, xn of b bytes each.
  2. For each chain i, start from a hash of the puzzle's parameters and salt, i and xi, and hash m times. Each step hashes the 32-byte state together with the salt and i. That is still one SHA-256 compression, and it makes every step specific to one chain of one puzzle. Call the end yi. All n chains run at the same time on different cores.
  3. Hide each link under a hash of the end of the previous chain: zi = (first b bytes of H(yi)) ⊕ xi+1, for i = 1, …, n − 1. Hashing the end means that a right guess of xi+1, which fixes these b bytes, says nothing about yi itself.
  4. Derive the key K by hashing the ends of all chains, y1, …, yn, in order, together with the parameters. Because every end goes into the key, a guessed link cannot be checked until the chains before it are done (section 4). Encrypt the transaction under K with authenticated encryption, and publish a commitment C, a 32-byte hash of K, so that anyone can check a claimed key with one hash.
  5. The puzzle is the parameters, the salt, x1, the hidden links z1, …, zn−1, and C.

Opening the puzzle (anyone)

Run chain 1 from the public x1 to get y1. Use it to unhide the next link, x2 = z1 ⊕ (first b bytes of H(y1)). Run chain 2, unhide x3, and so on. After all n chains, derive K from y1, …, yn and check it against C.

Why it works

  • Opening is sequential. Chain i + 1 cannot start until its link is known, and that link is hidden by the end of chain i. So opening takes N = n·m hashes one after another, however much hardware the opener has, unless it guesses a link (section 4).
  • Building is parallel. The sender knows every link from the start, so it runs all n chains at once. Its wall time is m steps on n cores, or N divided by its total hash rate. There is also one hash to start each chain and two at the end, which is negligible.
  • Size and delay are separate. The puzzle consists of the n links, n·b bytes, plus 68 bytes for the parameters, the salt and the commitment. The delay depends on N, so longer chains give a longer delay with the same puzzle size.

Worked example: the reference parameters

n = 512 chains of m = 225 hashes with 16-byte links: 234 hashes in total and an 8,260-byte puzzle. An attacker that computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns needs 60 seconds to open it. Measured on an M4 MacBook Air: building took 36.2 seconds on all 10 cores, and opening took 6.6 minutes on one core. The opened key matched the commitment and decrypted the transaction.

4. Security

Secrecy until the deadline rests on two assumptions about SHA-256 and two about the attacker's hardware. This section states them, gives the delay they imply, explains how we price the one hardware number that matters most, and says what happens if any of the numbers is wrong. It then goes through the attacks one by one.

The delay claim

Claim (not yet proved)

Model the hashes as one random oracle, and write N = n · m. Let an attacker make at most Q oracle calls in total, in sequential rounds that may each contain any number of calls in parallel. Then for every d ≥ 1, the probability that it makes the key-derivation call within N − d rounds is at most about

Q / (min(d, m) · 28b) + (Q2 + Q · N) / 2256.

Every call depends on one puzzle's salt, so the bound also holds when Q counts the calls on many puzzles together, and it then bounds the expected number of puzzles opened early.

Assumptions A2 and A3 turn rounds and calls into seconds. Write T = N · ℓ, and let the attacker make Q = R · T calls. Opening a puzzle even one chain early, at (1 − 1/n) · T, then has probability about R · T / (m · 28b), the figure in Table 2 below; at n = 512 one chain is 0.12 s of a one-minute delay. The claim gives the same figure for every larger saving, since it charges each guess only one chain (see below). Larger savings are surely harder, but we state no smaller figure for them. Until the key-derivation call is made, K is uniformly random to the attacker, and A4 then keeps the message hidden. The honest opener makes N + 3n + 1 hash calls: the chain steps; a start, a mask and an accumulator hash per chain, less one mask; and two hashes at the end.

Why the bound should hold. With fewer than N rounds, the attacker cannot run every chain after the previous one, so it must start at least one chain from a link it has not unhidden. Each hidden link is b uniformly random bytes, masked by a hash of the end of a chain the attacker has not finished, so a guess is right with probability 2−8b, and a right guess reveals part of that hash but nothing about the end itself. The key needs the end of every chain, so a run from a guessed link xk leads to the key only together with the ends of chains 1 to k − 1. Once the attacker has those, it also knows the true xk, and a right guess saves only the rounds the run has already spent. A guess that saves d rounds has therefore been run blind for d rounds. Runs from different guesses of the same link share no calls in their first chain, since they start from different 256-bit states. Once two runs reach the same later link, though, they need the same calls from then on, and the attacker can make those calls once. So a guessed run that gains a rounds has cost at least min(a, m) calls of its own. An attack can combine several gains, for instance one guessed chain with short prepared starts for every possible value of another link, so this per-run cost does not by itself prove the claim. It is the reason we expect the claim to hold. One right guess lets two parts of the sequence of chains run side by side, which saves at most half of the rounds. Saving more needs further work done ahead, either more right guesses or tables over all 28b values of a link, and the claim does not price that work separately. Every other shortcut means predicting a 32-byte oracle output, hitting a state on the honest chain, or finding a collision, which the second term covers.

Status. If the claim holds, it is tight up to a constant factor for savings of up to one chain, the case Table 2 counts: guessing ahead, below, achieves it. For longer savings it may be loose. The audit built two attackers against 1- and 2-byte links that defeat two sharper formulas. One charged each guess its whole saving d, and fails against an attacker that merges runs reaching the same link (round 7). The other squared the chance for savings over half, and fails against an attacker that prepares short starts for every value of a link (round 8). Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan prove that this construction with full-length links (their section 3.1) needs about n · m sequential rounds against attackers that make polynomially many calls. Their proof gives no explicit constants, and it does not cover short links, whose guessing term is the one that matters here. A weaker statement is proved for chainseal's exact encoding (SPEC.md section 8), following the soundness argument of Pietrzak and Reyzin for proofs of sequential work: finishing even one round early needs a right guess of some link, with probability at most Q / 28b plus terms of order Q · N / 2256. It lacks the factor min(d, m), so it grows with the delay: with 16-byte links and R = 1021 it is about 2−52 for a one-minute puzzle and 2−33 for a year. The claim itself is not proved (section 9). The rest of this paper uses the claim as stated.

Pricing the hardware number

A2 is the assumption that most needs physical evidence, and it can be checked against how SHA-256 is computed. One SHA-256 compression is 64 rounds, and each round needs the exact output of the previous one. The new state words are sums of up to five 32-bit terms. Some of them, such as the round constant and the message word, can be added ahead of time, but the next round applies rotations and bitwise functions to the exact bits of the result. So every round must finish at least one 32-bit addition with full carry propagation, plus a few levels of logic. Lee, Chan and Verbauwhede show that this much is also enough: with carry-save adders and retiming, the loop through one round needs one carry-propagate addition, one carry-save layer and the choice function Ch. Pipelining raises throughput but not the latency of one chain, since each step starts from the previous step's output. Unrolling removes the registers between rounds, so the relevant floor is the one for logic alone. So ℓ is roughly 64 times the shortest critical path of one round, measured over many unrolled rounds.

The assumption as an advantage factor. We state A2 relative to the fastest published design. Let ℓpub = 13.0 ns, the 7 nm core of Baldanzi et al., and say that an attacker has advantage A if it computes one chained hash in ℓ = ℓpub / A. This paper's design point is A ≈ 3.7, that is ℓ = 3.5 ns: 6.7 times faster than the M4 we measured and about 5 times faster than the best case for Zen 5. Some advantage over ℓpub is certain. That core is a general-purpose design, synthesized at one clock cycle per round with a register between rounds, and it makes no use of the fact that the last 20 bytes of a chain step's input are constant. A chip built for chains could unroll rounds, drop most of the registers and precompute the constant part of the message schedule. How large A can get is the weakest point of the analysis. The model row of the table puts numbers on this. Its unit is the FO4, the delay of an inverter driving four copies of itself. It prices the round loop of Lee et al. at 10–17 FO4: 7–12 for a fast 32-bit adder (Harris and Sutherland) and 3–5 that we assume for the carry-save layer and Ch. Predicted FO4 delays for 3–5 nm processes are 4.3–4.7 ps (Nikolić et al.). Then 64 rounds take 64 × 10 × 4.3 ps = 2.75 ns to 64 × 17 × 4.7 ps = 5.11 ns, and with 0.1–0.3 ns for the final addition and startup, 2.85–5.41 ns. Our allowance of 1 to 1.8 times for wires, cell choice and registers gives 2.9–9.7 ns, that is A ≈ 1.3–4.5. As a cross-check, Intel's mining patent describes a state update 13 logic levels deep (Suresh et al.); if each level cost one FO4, which is optimistic, a hash would take 3.6–3.9 ns. A process shrink alone gives only A ≈ 1.3. At 3.5 ns a round has 55 ps, about 12 FO4, so the design point needs a very good custom datapath and sits near the aggressive end of the model. The model's lower end is a conditional estimate, not a physical bound, and no published design that we found comes close to it. Work on verifiable delay functions faced the same question and also answered it with a stated factor: Drake proposed Amax = 10 as a conservative parameter for RSA-based VDFs, hoping that public ASICs would bring it to about 5. Here A = 10 means ℓ = 1.3 ns, about 4.5 FO4 per round, outside the model but not ruled out. Section 9 lists a careful estimate as the most important open question. A sealed transaction becomes public T seconds after it is sent, so only hardware that exists during that window matters, and there is nothing to gain by attacking old puzzles later. Opening early

  • Guess ahead. The attacker can guess a hidden link xk and run chains k, k + 1, … from the guess while its own chains 1 to k − 1 are still running. The key needs the end of every chain, so the guess cannot be checked against the commitment C. It is confirmed only when the attacker's own chains reach link k and reveal xk, and a right guess then saves the time it has already run. Each guess is right with probability 2−8b and costs at least one chain of hashes, or its saving if that is less than a chain, and the claim above is that saving d rounds, by this or any other attack, succeeds with probability at most about Q / (min(d, m) · 28b). Runs from different guesses can reach the same later link and then share the rest of their hashes, so a guess that runs several chains can cost less than the rounds it saves. Sharing grows with the number of guesses and with the number of chains each run covers, but it never reaches a run's first chain, which is all the claim charges. Savings of less than a chain are likelier but worth less; Table 2 counts the saving of one whole chain, from guesses that each run one chain. Guesses are independent, so this attack parallelizes perfectly, and it is what sets the link width. Both choices of section 3 are needed for this. Had the key been derived from the last chain's end alone, a guess of xn could be checked against C after one chain: the same chance would then open the puzzle after T / n instead of saving T / n. Had the mask been the raw end, a right guess of a 32-byte link would give away the whole previous end, and the chain that produced it could be skipped.

A fleet here is any hardware that computes R chain steps per second with at most T / m ≈ 1.8 µs per step, since a guessed chain can start as soon as the puzzle is published and only has to end before the attacker's own chains reach it. The ratio T / m = n · ℓ does not depend on the delay. GPUs at about 1 µs per step would qualify, and 10¹⁸ H/s of them is about 37 million RTX 5090s. Mining chips are hard-wired to block headers and cannot run chains, but a chip like them could be built for chains. For scale, 10¹⁸ hashes per second of today's mining machines costs about $10M, but mining machines cannot run chains, and 10²¹ is roughly the whole Bitcoin network. "Per year" assumes the fleet attacks nonstop and always has puzzles to attack. In general, to keep this attack's chance below 2−λ per puzzle against a fleet of R hashes per second, take 8b ≥ log2(R · T / m) + λ bits. Against 10²¹ H/s with λ = 64 that is 115 bits, so at least 15 bytes; 16 bytes leave a margin. If the claim above holds, no strategy does much better than this one; for short links it is not yet proved (section 9).

An early opening is a bounded harm, not a break. A puzzle opened one chain early is open 0.12 s before its minute is up, and the claim bounds every larger saving by the same chance. So even if every successful guess gave away the whole delay, the expected time lost per puzzle would be at most the chance times T: about 3.5 × 10⁻²² s with 16-byte links against 10²¹ H/s, against 1.4 × 10⁻¹² s with 12-byte links. With 16 bytes the guessing term is cryptographically small, so the remaining uncertainty in the delay is the attacker's speed ℓ, not guessing. Each extra byte costs n bytes of puzzle, 512 bytes at the reference parameters.

  • Guess the key. A guess of the 32-byte key is checked against C with one hash and is right with chance 2−256.
  • Precompute. Every puzzle draws a fresh random salt, and every step depends on it, so chains computed for one puzzle are of no use for another, except with negligible probability. Once a puzzle is public, an attacker could compute chain segments for it from states of its choice, hoping the honest chain passes through one. This is the Q · N / 2256 term of the claim.
  • Use a quantum computer. Quantum search needs a way to recognize the right link. A candidate for xk can be recognized only by running chain k and the chains after it to the key, and only once the chains before k are done. Each of the roughly 24b search steps then costs at least one whole chain in sequence, far more than running the chains honestly, and many quantum computers in parallel cut the number of steps only by the square root of their number. The idealized construction is proved secure against quantum openers (Afshar et al. 2023); the variant with short links has not been analyzed separately.
  • Get the puzzle early. The attacker's clock starts when the puzzle is published. A sender who builds puzzles in advance must keep them private until use.
  • Lie about the key, or replace the message. A claimed key is checked against C with one hash, and authenticated decryption then checks the transaction, so a wrong key is rejected at once. Once the key is known, though, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle. Whatever orders the transactions must therefore commit to the whole sealed transaction, for example by its hash, before the key comes out. Nothing checks in advance that a puzzle is well formed; see spam in section 8.

5. What hashes can and cannot do

The impossibility results below are proved in the random-oracle model: the hash is a random function that anyone can call, many times in parallel, and delay is counted in rounds of calls. Turning rounds into seconds takes the hardware assumption of section 4. Items marked as not proved or not analyzed are not theorems.

Possible with hashes alone
  • A per-transaction time lock with any delay, costing the sender work in proportion to the delay.
  • A puzzle whose size does not depend on the delay: about n · b bytes.
  • Security against known quantum attacks (proved for the idealized puzzle, not for this instantiation).
  • One puzzle covering many transactions from the same sender.
  • Building outsourced to k services: each builds a puzzle and returns it with its key, and the sender encrypts under a hash of all k keys. The key stays secret if any one service is honest, the delay is unchanged, and size and opening work grow k-fold. Any one service can still spoil the result by sending a bad puzzle. Not analyzed formally.
  • Commit–reveal in which withholding the key delays a transaction with a well-formed puzzle but cannot cancel it.
Not possible with hashes alone
  • A provable delay longer than the sender's total hashing, run one hash after another.
  • Delay encryption: senders encrypt cheaply to a slot, and one computation opens all of it (proved when the setup is cheap).
  • A small puzzle for a highly parallel sender (believed, not proved).

The delay is limited by the sender's work

Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) proved the following. If building a puzzle takes N hash calls in total, an attacker can open it in about N rounds, where in each round it may compute any number of hashes in parallel. The intuition: whatever makes the puzzle solvable is hidden among the N hashes the sender computed. In each round the attacker guesses a complete run of the sender that fits the puzzle and everything learned so far, and computes all of that run's hashes at once. Each round either opens the puzzle or uncovers at least one more hash the real sender used, and there are only N of those.

This attack is expensive. Each round makes a huge number of hash calls, and the version with N rounds also needs unlimited computation between rounds; an efficient version needs about N/ε rounds to succeed except with probability ε. So the result is a limit on what any proof can promise, not a cheap way to open puzzles. The chained puzzle needs about N rounds, so no hash-only puzzle can provably do better.

In practice: no proof can promise that a hash-only puzzle keeps an attacker busy for longer than the sender's total number of hashes, run one after another at the attacker's speed. So a one-minute lock against a 3.5 ns attacker costs the sender at least 234 hashes, spread over as many cores as it likes. The chained puzzle pays exactly this price.

Delay encryption is out of reach

Suppose a public setup costs S hash calls, after which senders can encrypt to slot 1234 with E hash calls, while computing the slot's key takes T seconds of sequential work. Then running the setup and encrypting a random message is itself a time-lock puzzle that costs S + E hash calls to build. By the result above, its message can be recovered in about S + E rounds. If the setup is cheap, the delay is limited by the cost of one encryption, which is the opposite of what delay encryption promises.

This argument does not settle the case of a long setup that itself uses the hash: then S + E is large and the bound says nothing useful. If, in addition, each slot has only one key that passes a quick public check, computing it is a verifiable delay function, and those are impossible from hashes alone even with an expensive private setup (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2024; Abusalah et al. 2025).

Batching and puzzle size

Independent puzzles share nothing, so solving one teaches nothing about another, and no method is known that opens several for less than a full sequential solve each. A sender can still put many transactions under one key and one puzzle. The version that would help a mempool, where unrelated senders encrypt privately to a common slot without knowing its key, is delay encryption, covered above.

On size: each chain the sender runs in parallel needs its own hidden link of about b bytes, so a chained puzzle that lets the sender work g ways in parallel has about g · b bytes. Whether some cleverer hash-only puzzle can be smaller is open. Lower bounds of this kind on communication are known only for restricted kinds of key exchange (Haitner et al. 2019), not for time-lock puzzles.

6. Implementation and measurements

chainseal implements section 3 as a complete, versioned format. A sender builds puzzles in advance and, when a transaction is ready, encrypts it under one. Anyone can then open the result in one of two ways: with the key, if the sender reveals it, or by solving the puzzle. A solve saves its progress every 10 seconds, so it can be stopped and resumed, and several puzzles can be solved at once, one per core. The code is about 4,000 lines of Rust, whose only dependencies are getrandom for the operating system's random numbers and signal-hook for stopping a solve cleanly. It has been run on macOS on an Apple M4 and on Linux on an AMD EPYC 9554P, and continuous integration builds and tests it on Linux for x86-64 and AArch64 and on macOS.

Encryption uses only SHA-256. The key gives an HMAC-SHA256 keystream, and a 32-byte HMAC tag covers the whole sealed transaction, so whoever lacks the key cannot change the puzzle or the ciphertext without the change being detected when the transaction is opened. A sealed transaction of L bytes takes 128 + n·b + L bytes:

Rows two to five are the puzzle, 68 + n·b bytes. One puzzle can seal several messages, each with its own nonce; they all open together.

How it was checked

  • A written specification fixes every byte of every format, with test vectors: 7 valid cases and 51 malformed inputs that an implementation must reject.
  • A second implementation, in Python, shares no code with the Rust one; it uses Python's own SHA-256 and HMAC and its own parsers. The two agree on every test vector, and each opens the other's sealed messages.
  • Tests. Known answers for SHA-256 and HMAC, every SHA-256 backend against plain SHA-256, round trips over many parameter sets, and end-to-end tests of the command-line tool, including interrupting and resuming a solve. A property test feeds every decoder 480,000 mutated encodings, and cargo-fuzz ran each decoder for two minutes under AddressSanitizer, about 108 million inputs in all; neither found a fault. The only unsafe code is in the two hardware backends.
  • Guessing ahead by one chain (section 4), run at toy sizes with 1-byte links and a budget of a quarter of all values: it saved a chain on 4,958 of 20,000 puzzles, against 5,000 ± 61 expected.
  • A security audit of the code and the specification by an AI model, GPT-6 Astra. It ran in nine rounds, each checking the fixes for the previous one. The first four found 10 blockers, 3 major findings, 12 minor ones and a nit, and the fifth found nothing new. Rounds 6 to 9 checked the change that derives the key from every chain end. They found that the raw mask leaked the previous end and a bug in resuming a finished solve, both fixed. They also built attackers against 1- and 2-byte links that broke two sharper versions of the guessing bound, which section 4 no longer claims. The ninth round found no blocker or major finding. The reports and the fixes are in the repository. The audit covered an earlier C implementation; the Rust one that replaced it reproduces its test vectors and command-line behaviour and was reviewed against it.

Limits of this evaluation. No human cryptographer has reviewed the code or the format. The x86 version, which uses the SHA-NI instructions, was built, tested and timed on one x86 machine, a 4-core AMD EPYC 9554P cloud VM. All other timings are from an Apple M4 laptop. The n = 64 and shorter-delay builds were timed with 12-byte links; the link width adds only n mask hashes, so it does not change the build time.

8. Using it in Ethereum

  • Commit–reveal with a fallback. The user sends the encrypted transaction, the puzzle and the commitment C. After ordering, the user reveals K, and nodes check H(K) = C. The ordering must commit to the whole sealed transaction, not just to C: once K is known, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle. Solving is needed only for users who stay silent, and a deposit can charge them for it. A deposit that is forfeited when a solve fails also deters cancelling with a malformed puzzle. Execution rules must say what a transaction opened late may still do.
  • Opening silent users' puzzles needs dedicated hardware. To open within about T, solvers need chips as fast as the attacker the delay is priced against. On laptops, a one-minute puzzle takes about 6.6 minutes. Either the network runs solver chips, or late-opened transactions execute in a later slot.
  • Solving cost. If every transaction in a 12-second slot of 200 had to be opened by solvers, with one-minute puzzles, that would keep about 1,000 attacker-speed chain circuits busy at all times, or about 6,700 M4 cores. In the normal case almost none are needed, since users reveal their keys.
  • Bandwidth. A signed ETH transfer is about 110 bytes. Sealed under a laptop puzzle (n = 64) it takes 1,262 bytes, about 11× more, and under the reference puzzle (n = 512) it takes 8,430 bytes, about 77× more. These sizes include the encryption's own 60 bytes.
  • Energy and phones. One puzzle took 36.2 s at an estimated 20 W on the laptop, about 0.2 Wh. That is 1% of a 20 Wh phone battery if a phone were as efficient; no phone was measured. Puzzles do not depend on the transaction, so a wallet can build a stock while charging and spend one per transaction. Phones can also outsource building to several services, as in section 5.
  • Spam. The bare construction gives no way to check, before solving, that a puzzle leads to its committed key, so a malformed puzzle is found only after a full solve. A proof system or a trusted builder could check it, at extra cost. Fees and deposits must be collectable without the key, and the number of open puzzles per sender should be capped.
  • Related designs. Threshold-encrypted mempools release keys from a committee: Shutter's keypers, Ferveo (Bebel and Ojha 2022) and FairBlock (Momeni, Gorbunov and Zhang 2022), with batched schemes that keep unselected transactions private (Choudhuri et al. 2024); Passerat-Palmbach (2026) surveys them. Radius's PVDE (2022) seals each transaction under an RSA time-lock puzzle with a zero-knowledge proof that the puzzle is well formed, and multiparty delay encryption (Khajehpour et al. 2023) opens a whole batch with one timed computation. Buterin proposed time-locked transactions as an alternative to commit–reveal in 2018, and later contrasted costly per-transaction time locks with delay encryption (2023, 2024). Voluntary opening backed by forced opening is the pattern of timed commitments (Boneh and Naor 2000). chainseal plays the role of PVDE's puzzle with a hash in place of RSA: it needs no setup and no committee, at the price of more work for the sender and a puzzle that cannot be checked in advance.

9. Open questions

For per-transaction time locks, no new construction is needed: the chained puzzle is optimal up to a constant factor among random-oracle constructions, the parameters are workable, and an implementation exists. What is missing is analysis, measurements on more hardware, and the network design. For delay encryption, new ideas are needed, and they must go beyond hashes, at least when the setup is cheap. The open work, roughly in order of importance:

  1. How fast can chained SHA-256 be? Every parameter is priced against this. A careful circuit-level estimate, ideally from chip designers, would turn the advantage factor A ≈ 3.7, that is 3.5 ns, into a defensible number. An alternative is a hash whose sequential speed differs less between laptops and custom chips.
  2. A concrete proof of the claim in section 4. One statement with explicit terms for attacker speed and hardware budget, link width, precomputation, the number of puzzles attacked, and quantum attackers, in a form a deployment could cite. It should promise secrecy until shortly before the nominal delay, say 0.9 T, except with a stated probability. The asymptotic results exist, and a weaker concrete bound is proved (section 4); the claim itself is not.
  3. Measurements beyond one laptop. chainseal has been timed only on an Apple M4 and on one x86 server core (37 ns per chained hash). Timing it on x86 desktops, on phones and on GPUs would replace the estimates in sections 4 and 7 with measurements.
  4. A lower bound on puzzle size. Chained puzzles need about b bytes per chain the sender runs in parallel. Does every hash-only time lock that lets the sender work g ways in parallel need size proportional to g, or is there a smaller puzzle?
  5. Mechanism design. Who runs solvers and how they are paid (a published key can be copied, so the reward cannot simply go to whoever posts it first), who pays for silent users, how late-opened transactions execute, how malformed puzzles are handled, and how the puzzle bytes fit the bandwidth budget.
  6. Delay encryption beyond hashes. Compare isogeny, lattice and batch-solvable constructions on setup, quantum security and cost against the hash-only baseline measured here.
