Time-Lock Encryption from Hashes Alone

A chained-hash puzzle for sealing transactions, what it costs, what hashes cannot do, and an implementation

Vitalik Buterin · Quang Dao · Justin Drake

Code, specification and benchmark logs: github.com/quangvdao/chainseal

Abstract

An encrypted mempool lets block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run. Each transaction must then open once it is ordered, without a committee that holds a decryption key and without letting its sender keep it sealed. A time-lock puzzle provides this: the transaction opens by itself after a fixed amount of sequential computation. Efficient time-lock puzzles use repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, which needs a trusted setup or is broken by quantum computers. Puzzles built from a hash function avoid both problems, but the sender must compute as many hashes as the opener, only in parallel, and Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (CRYPTO 2011) showed that this cost is unavoidable. We ask whether it is affordable for sealing a transaction.

We present chainseal, which instantiates the chained-hash puzzle of Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan with SHA-256: the sender computes several hash chains in parallel and links them so that the opener must compute them in sequence. In the random-oracle model, we prove that opening a puzzle even one round early requires guessing a 24-byte value; against the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network, the chance is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock. Turning rounds into seconds requires an assumption about hardware. Against an attacker who computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns, 3.7 times faster than the fastest published design, a one-minute lock costs the sender 234 hashes, 42.1 s on the CPU of an Apple M4 laptop, and adds 512 bytes to the transaction. The puzzle does not depend on the transaction, so this work can be done in advance; at the hash rate we measured on the GPU of a larger laptop (an M4 Max), building thousands of puzzles at once would take 5.8 s each. Finally, we sketch how chainseal could serve as a fallback for commit–reveal in Ethereum: senders would normally reveal their keys after ordering, and a sender who withholds its key could only delay its transaction, not cancel it, as long as its puzzle is well formed.

1. Introduction

In an encrypted mempool, block builders order transactions before they can read them, which removes their ability to front-run. The hard part is opening the transactions afterwards. A committee can hold a decryption key, but then a majority of the committee must be trusted. The sender can reveal its own key after ordering, but then the sender sees the ordering first and can withhold the key when the ordering does not suit it. A time lock avoids both: the transaction opens by itself once a fixed amount of computation has been done, and nobody can do that computation much faster by buying more machines.

There are two versions of this idea, and they behave very differently when only a hash function is available.

Time-lock puzzle

The sender picks a key, encrypts the transaction under it, and attaches a puzzle whose solution is the key. Anyone can solve the puzzle, but it takes T seconds of computation that cannot be parallelized. Each sender attaches its own puzzle, and each puzzle is solved separately.

Delay encryption

Senders encrypt to a public label, such as a slot number (in Ethereum, a slot is the 12-second interval in which one block is proposed). After the slot, one T-second computation on that label yields a key that opens every transaction sent to it. Encryption is cheap and the network solves once per slot.

Time-lock puzzles are known from repeated squaring in RSA or class groups, and newer candidates come from lattices. Delay encryption is known from isogenies. The group and isogeny constructions are broken by quantum computers, and the RSA and isogeny ones need a trusted setup. This note asks what a hash function alone can do. Throughout, the hash is SHA-256.

Two observations make a hash-only time lock possible at all. First, the secrecy only needs to be short-lived. The classical barriers say hashes cannot give public-key encryption that stays secure forever against every efficient attacker, but a time lock only has to hold for a minute against an attacker with a fixed hardware budget, and it is meant to open afterwards. Second, the sender and the opener hash in different ways: the sender can hash in parallel, while the opener must hash in sequence. The construction turns the sender's parallel width into the opener's delay.

Summary of results

  1. Time-lock puzzles work. A set of hash chains linked as in section 3 gives a per-transaction time lock. Sealing a transaction for one minute against a very fast attacker costs the sender 234 SHA-256 hashes, spread over all its cores. That took 42.1 seconds on a laptop, and the sealed transaction is 512 bytes longer. There is no trusted setup and no committee, and known quantum attacks are far too slow to matter.
  2. The delay is proved, given one hardware number. In the random-oracle model, we prove that an opener recovers the key in fewer sequential rounds than the honest opener only by guessing a 24-byte value or finding a collision. Against the hash rate of the whole Bitcoin network, the chance is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock and 2−97 for a one-year lock (section 4). The proof is written out in the specification and has not been checked by machine or by independent reviewers. Turning rounds into seconds needs ℓ, the fastest time in which anyone can compute one chained SHA-256. We state ℓ as an advantage factor A over the fastest published SHA-256 core, 13.0 ns per hash, and price at A ≈ 3.7, that is ℓ = 3.5 ns. A circuit model built from published round analyses and adder delays puts a chip designed for chains at A ≈ 1.3–4.5, but no such chip has been built. If ℓ is misjudged by a factor k, the delay shrinks by k and nothing else breaks. How large A can be is the least certain number in this paper, so section 7 shows the cost of every choice.
  3. The sender's cost cannot be avoided, but it can be paid early. The sender must compute as many hashes as the attacker computes in sequence, only in parallel. For constructions from a random oracle this is optimal up to a constant factor (section 5). In time, a sender spends about 0.70 seconds of building per second of delay on the laptop's CPU (measured), A GPU helps only when building thousands of puzzles at once, and then mostly on a large one: from measured hash rates, the ratio is 0.4 on the laptop's GPU and 0.097 on an M4 Max GPU. Puzzles do not depend on the transaction, so a wallet can build them while idle and keep a stock (section 7).
  4. Delay encryption is out of reach. Encrypting to a slot so that one computation opens all its transactions is impossible with hashes alone when the setup is cheap. With an expensive setup the question is open. No known method opens puzzles from different senders for less than one full solve each.
  5. The best use is as a fallback. Behind commit–reveal, users normally reveal their keys after ordering, and puzzles are solved only for those who stay silent. If its puzzle is well formed, a user who dislikes the ordering can then delay its transaction but not cancel it (section 8).
  6. An implementation exists. chainseal is a complete, specified format with a Rust implementation, a second implementation in Python, test vectors and benchmarks (section 6).

2. Background

Hash chains

A hash chain starts from a value and hashes it N times in a row. Each step needs the previous output, so if the hash has no shortcut and its outputs cannot be guessed, even unlimited hardware needs N steps one after another. A single chain would make a fine lock, except that its builder also needs N sequential steps: a one-minute lock would take the sender a minute on the fastest hardware there is, and much longer on a phone. The construction in section 3 fixes this by building many short chains at the same time and linking them in a fixed order.

Merkle puzzles, and why they do not help

The classical hash-based way to keep a secret from an eavesdropper is Merkle's 1974 puzzles. Alice publishes k puzzles, each breakable with about k work. Bob breaks one at random and announces its identifier, and the two share its secret. The eavesdropper does not know which puzzle Bob chose, so she must break about half of them, about k2 work in total.

Impagliazzo and Rudich (1989) showed that key exchange secure against every efficient eavesdropper cannot be built from a random oracle in a black-box way, and Barak and Mahmoody (2009) that in the random-oracle model Merkle's quadratic gap is the best possible. For a time lock, though, a gap in total work is worth nothing: the eavesdropper's k2 is k separate pieces of work, each as large as Bob's, so on k machines it takes Bob's time. An attacker who can rent hardware and only needs to win a one-minute race does exactly that. What helps is a gap in sequential time, which a chain provides.

Prior work

Theory. Rivest, Shamir and Wagner (1996) introduced time-lock puzzles, based on repeated squaring in an RSA group; Bitansky et al. (2016) built them from randomized encodings. Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) studied time-lock puzzles in the random-oracle model, which treats the hash as a random function anyone can query. They gave the chained puzzle of section 3, including a variant that iterates the hash in each link so that the puzzle stays small for a long delay (their section 3.1). They also proved that no puzzle in this model can force more sequential rounds than the sender's total number of hash calls, up to a constant factor. Two neighbouring notions need different things. A proof of sequential work shows that time has passed, but its creator knows no secret answer in advance (Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan 2013; Cohen and Pietrzak 2018). A verifiable delay function needs a unique output that anyone can check quickly (Boneh et al. 2018), and that is impossible from a random oracle alone (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2025; Abusalah et al. 2026). Concrete bounds exist for computing a full-width hash chain with parallel queries, classically and against quantum attackers (Chung et al. 2021; Blocki, Lee and Zhou 2021). They cover a single chain, not several chains linked through masked secrets as in chainseal.

Practice. The chained-hash idea has been implemented several times. Gwern Branwen's 2011 essay described it. Dorian Johnson's Go tool (2013) builds SHA-512 chains in parallel, with full 64-byte seeds, each encrypted under the end of the previous chain with AES. Peter Todd's timelock (2014) uses SHA-256 chains, and Todd published a 32-chain puzzle with Bitcoin bounties on its intermediate secrets. Kadupul (Skjegstad, Madhavapeddy and Crowcroft 2014) uses Todd's puzzles to reward packet forwarding. EtherDream's Timelock Online (2024) builds chains in a browser on CPUs and GPUs, with 4-byte seeds and a salt per seed. Its slow hash is PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA-256, and, as in chainseal, its key depends on the end of every chain (section 4). Separately, Solana's Proof of History uses sequential SHA-256 as a clock rather than for encryption (Yakovenko 2018); Shoup (2022) discusses how its elapsed time depends on hardware. None of these states a concrete security bound or measures costs against an explicit attacker model.

This note. Neither the construction nor its practical use is new. What this note adds is a concrete security proof for chainseal's exact encoding together with its sensitivity to the hardware numbers (section 4), parameters for sealing transactions with costs measured on current CPUs and GPUs (sections 6 and 7), a specified and tested implementation (section 6), and its use as a fallback for commit–reveal (section 8).

3. The construction

The sender builds n chains of m hashes each, all at once, and links them so that an opener can only run them in order. Its parameters:

nnumber of chains16
mhashes per chain230 ≈ 1.1 billion
bbytes per link, the secret that starts each chain32
N = n·mtotal hashes, which sets the delay234 ≈ 17 billion

We call n = 16, m = 230, b = 24 the reference parameters. They give a one-minute delay against an attacker at 3.5 ns per hash, with a 452-byte puzzle. Sections 4 and 7 explain the choice: the delay fixes N; 24-byte links are the shortest for which the proved bound stays negligible for any practical delay; and n only trades puzzle size against how many cores one puzzle can use.

Building the puzzle (the sender)

  1. Pick a random 16-byte salt and n random links x1, …, xn of b bytes each.
  2. For each chain i, start from a hash of the puzzle's parameters and salt, i and xi, and hash m times. Each step hashes the 32-byte state together with the salt and i. That is still one SHA-256 compression, and it makes every step specific to one chain of one puzzle. Call the end yi. All n chains run at the same time on different cores.
  3. Hide each link under a hash of the end of the previous chain: zi = (first b bytes of H(yi)) ⊕ xi+1, for i = 1, …, n − 1. Hashing the end, rather than using it directly, means that learning xi+1 says nothing about yi itself.
  4. Derive the key K by hashing the ends of all chains, y1, …, yn, in order, together with the parameters. Because every end goes into the key, an opener cannot leave out a chain even if it somehow learns the link that follows it. Encrypt the transaction under K with authenticated encryption, and publish a commitment C, a 32-byte hash of K, so that anyone can check a claimed key with one hash.
  5. The puzzle is the parameters, the salt, x1, the hidden links z1, …, zn−1, and C.

Opening the puzzle (anyone)

Run chain 1 from the public x1 to get y1. Use it to unhide the next link, x2 = z1 ⊕ (first b bytes of H(y1)). Run chain 2, unhide x3, and so on. After all n chains, derive K from y1, …, yn and check it against C.

Why it works

  • Opening is sequential. Chain i + 1 cannot start until its link is known, and that link is hidden by the end of chain i. So opening takes N = n·m hashes one after another, however much hardware the opener has. Section 4 proves this in the random-oracle model.
  • Building is parallel. The sender knows every link from the start, so it runs all n chains at once. With a core for every chain its wall time is m steps; in general it is about N divided by its total hash rate. The few extra hashes per chain, to start it and hide its link, and the two at the end are negligible.
  • Size and delay are separate. The puzzle consists of the n links, n·b bytes, plus 68 bytes for the parameters, the salt and the commitment. The delay depends on N, so longer chains give a longer delay with the same puzzle size.

Worked example: the reference parameters

n = 16 chains of m = 230 hashes with 24-byte links: 234 hashes in total and a 452-byte puzzle. An attacker that computes one chained SHA-256 every 3.5 ns needs 60 seconds to open it. Measured on an M4 MacBook Air: building took 42.1 seconds on all 10 cores, and opening took 6 min 42 s on one core. The opened key matched the commitment and decrypted the transaction.

4. Security

Secrecy until the deadline rests on two assumptions about SHA-256 and two about the attacker's hardware. This section states them, proves the delay in the random-oracle model, explains how we price the one hardware number that the proof cannot supply, and says what happens if any of the numbers is wrong. It then goes through the attacks one by one and ends with the choice of link width.

The delay theorem

Theorem (random-oracle model)

Model the chain step and the other labelled hashes as independent random oracles, and count time in rounds: in one round an attacker sends any number of queries at once and then receives all their answers. The honest opener obtains K after n(m + 2) + 1 rounds. Let P = n(m + 3) + 1, the number of hashes the honest opener computes. An attacker that receives the puzzle and makes at most Q queries in total outputs K in fewer rounds with probability at most

Q / 28b + (3QP + P2 + Q + 1) / 2256.

The bound counts every query, including any made before the puzzle was published. With 24-byte links the first term, the chance of guessing a link, is the largest; for a one-minute lock the others are smaller by a factor of about 228.

Why it holds. Call the 32-byte values the honest opener computes, in order, the honest path: each chain's start value, its m states, its mask and its accumulator value, and then K. Each value on the path is the answer to a query whose input contains the value before it, or, for the start of chain i, the link xi, which the puzzle hides under the mask of chain i − 1. An attacker can learn a value on the path before its predecessor in only three ways. It can send an input that contains a path value it has not yet seen, which means guessing 32 bytes. It can receive a path value as the answer to some other query, which is a collision. Or it can start a chain from a link whose mask it has not yet seen, which means guessing the b-byte link. The first two have probability at most (3QP + P2) / 2256 together, and the third at most Q / 28b. Without them, every value on the path becomes known only in the round after its predecessor, exactly as for the honest opener, and an attacker that outputs K without knowing it is right with probability at most (Q + 1) / 2256, counting its tests against C. The argument follows the soundness proof of Pietrzak and Reyzin for proofs of sequential work (their Lemmas 6 and 9), and SPEC.md section 8 writes it out for chainseal's exact encoding. It has not been machine-checked or reviewed by a third party.

What it means in seconds. Assumptions A2 and A3 turn rounds and queries into time. Write T = n · m · ℓ, and let the attacker make Q = R · T queries. Against R = 1021 hashes per second, roughly the whole Bitcoin network, the bound is about 2−116 for a one-minute lock and 2−97 for a one-year lock. It depends on the number of chains only through P ≈ N, which the delay fixes. It covers every saving, however small: except with this probability, the attacker does not finish even one round before the honest opener. Until then K is uniformly random to the attacker, and A4 keeps the message hidden.

The attacker's total hash rate R is easier to price, because the hardware exists. A Bitcoin miner (S21 XP) computes 270 TH/s for about $2.8k, an RTX 5090 GPU 27 GH/s (hashcat), and the Bitcoin network about 1021 H/s (October 2026). A mining pipeline finishes one double SHA-256 per clock cycle, every 3 ns in a 2013 chip at about 335 MHz (Taylor 2013), but each hash spends many cycles in the pipeline. These machines compute many independent hashes at once and say little about how fast one hash can follow another, so they bound R in A3, not ℓ; we take R = 1021 H/s.

Pricing the hardware number

A2 is the assumption that most needs physical evidence, and it can be checked against how SHA-256 is computed. One SHA-256 compression is 64 rounds, and each round needs the exact output of the previous one. The new state words are sums of up to five 32-bit terms. Some of them, such as the round constant and the message word, can be added ahead of time, but the next round applies rotations and bitwise functions to the exact bits of the result. So every round must finish at least one 32-bit addition with full carry propagation, plus a few levels of logic. Lee, Chan and Verbauwhede show that this much is also enough: with carry-save adders and retiming, the loop through one round needs one carry-propagate addition, one carry-save layer and the choice function Ch. Pipelining raises throughput but not the latency of one chain, since each step starts from the previous step's output. Unrolling removes the registers between rounds, so the relevant floor is the one for logic alone. So ℓ is roughly 64 times the shortest critical path of one round, measured over many unrolled rounds.

This paper's design point is A ≈ 3.7, that is ℓ = 3.5 ns: 6.6 times faster than the M4 we measured and about 5 times faster than the best case for Zen 5. Some advantage over ℓpub is certain. That core is a general-purpose design, synthesized at one clock cycle per round with a register between rounds, and it makes no use of the fact that the last 20 bytes of a chain step's input are constant. A chip built for chains could unroll rounds, drop most of the registers and precompute the constant part of the message schedule.

The model row of the table puts numbers on this. Its unit is the FO4, the delay of an inverter driving four copies of itself. It prices the round loop of Lee et al. at 10–17 FO4: 7–12 for a fast 32-bit adder (Harris and Sutherland) and 3–5 that we assume for the carry-save layer and Ch. Predicted FO4 delays for 3–5 nm processes are 4.3–4.7 ps (Nikolić et al.). Then 64 rounds take 64 × 10 × 4.3 ps = 2.75 ns to 64 × 17 × 4.7 ps = 5.11 ns, and with 0.1–0.3 ns for the final addition and startup, 2.85–5.41 ns. Our allowance of 1 to 1.8 times for wires, cell choice and registers gives 2.9–9.7 ns, that is A ≈ 1.3–4.5. As a cross-check, Intel's mining patent describes a state update 13 logic levels deep (Suresh et al.); if each level cost one FO4, which is optimistic, a hash would take 3.6–3.9 ns. A process shrink alone gives only A ≈ 1.3. At 3.5 ns a round has 55 ps, about 12 FO4, so the design point needs a very good custom datapath and sits near the aggressive end of the model. The model's lower end is a conditional estimate, not a physical bound, and no published design that we found comes close to it.

Work on verifiable delay functions faced the same question and also answered it with a stated factor: Drake proposed Amax = 10 as a conservative parameter for RSA-based VDFs, hoping that public ASICs would bring it to about 5. Here A = 10 means ℓ = 1.3 ns, about 4.5 FO4 per round, outside the model but not ruled out. Section 9 lists a careful estimate as the most important open question. We do not claim that 3.5 ns is a lower bound: the theorem holds whatever ℓ the reader believes, and Table 2 in section 7 shows what each choice costs the sender.

So among the hardware numbers, only ℓ matters at this scale: the delay is exactly as good as its estimate. The secrecy is also short-lived, which limits the damage of any mistake. A sealed transaction becomes public T seconds after it is sent, so only hardware that exists during that window matters, and there is nothing to gain by attacking old puzzles later. A faster chip next year shortens next year's delays, not this year's. As with key lengths, ℓ should be repriced as hardware improves.

Attacks

  • Guess a link. An attacker can guess a hidden link xk and run chains k, k + 1, … from the guess while its own chains 1 to k − 1 are still running. This is the attack behind the first term of the theorem. With 24-byte links each guess is right with probability 2−192. Two choices of section 3 also limit what a right guess would buy, which would matter only for shorter links (Appendix A). Because the key needs the end of every chain, a guess cannot be checked against the commitment C until the attacker's own chains reach link k; had the key been derived from the last chain's end alone, a guess of xn could be checked after one chain, and a right guess would open the puzzle after T / n. Because the mask is a hash of the previous end rather than the end itself, a right guess of a link reveals nothing about the chain before it.
  • Guess the key. A guess of the 32-byte key is checked against C with one hash and is right with chance 2−256.
  • Precompute. Every puzzle draws a fresh random salt, and every hash on the honest path depends on it together with a fresh link or a 32-byte value computed from one. So work done before a puzzle is published, or on another puzzle, is of no use, except with the negligible probability the theorem already counts. Once a puzzle is public, an attacker could compute chain segments for it from states of its choice, hoping the honest chain passes through one. That is the 3QP / 2256 term.
  • Use a quantum computer. Quantum search needs a way to recognize the right link. A candidate for xk can be recognized only by running chain k and the chains after it to the key, and only once the chains before k are done. With 24-byte links, each of the roughly 296 search steps then costs at least one whole chain in sequence, far more than running the chains honestly. The idealized construction is proved secure against quantum openers (Afshar et al. 2023); chainseal's encoding has not been analyzed separately.
  • Get the puzzle early. The attacker's clock starts when the puzzle is published. A sender who builds puzzles in advance must keep them private until use.
  • Lie about the key, or replace the message. A claimed key is checked against C with one hash, and authenticated decryption then checks the transaction, so a wrong key is rejected at once. Once the key is known, though, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle, so the ordering must bind the whole sealed transaction (section 8). Nothing checks in advance that a puzzle is well formed; see spam in section 8.

Link width

The first term of the theorem, Q / 28b, is the chance of guessing a link, and it is the only place where the link width enters. Shorter links make the puzzle smaller, but they weaken the bound, and more so for longer delays:

We use 24-byte links, the shortest for which the bound stays negligible for any practical delay. With 32-byte links the guessing term falls below the other terms, so the link width drops out of the bound. That adds about 29 bits of margin for a one-minute lock and 10 for a one-year lock, at a cost of 128 bytes per puzzle, 17% of a sealed transfer. It does not protect against underestimating the attacker: both terms grow in proportion to Q, so an attacker k times stronger than assumed costs the same log2 k bits with either width. This note relies only on the theorem. Appendix A discusses a sharper bound for 16-byte links, which we do not use.

5. What hashes can and cannot do

The impossibility results below are proved in the random-oracle model: the hash is a random function that anyone can call, many times in parallel, and delay is counted in rounds of calls. Turning rounds into seconds takes the hardware assumption of section 4. Items marked as not proved or not analyzed are not theorems.

Possible with hashes alone
  • A per-transaction time lock with any delay, costing the sender work in proportion to the delay.
  • A puzzle whose size does not depend on the delay: about n · b bytes.
  • Security against known quantum attacks (proved for the idealized puzzle, not for this instantiation).
  • One puzzle covering many transactions from the same sender.
  • Building outsourced to k services: each builds a puzzle and returns it with its key, and the sender encrypts under a hash of all k keys. The key stays secret if any one service is honest, the delay is unchanged, and size and opening work grow k-fold. Any one service can still spoil the result by sending a bad puzzle. Not analyzed formally.
  • Commit–reveal in which withholding the key delays a transaction with a well-formed puzzle but cannot cancel it.
  • Open: whether a puzzle smaller than about g · b bytes exists for a sender working g ways in parallel (section 9).
Not possible with hashes alone
  • A provable delay longer than the sender's total hashing, run one hash after another.
  • Delay encryption: senders encrypt cheaply to a slot, and one computation opens all of it (proved when the setup is cheap).

The delay is limited by the sender's work

Mahmoody, Moran and Vadhan (2011) proved the following. If building a puzzle takes N hash calls in total, an attacker can open it in about N rounds, where in each round it may compute any number of hashes in parallel. The intuition: whatever makes the puzzle solvable is hidden among the N hashes the sender computed. In each round the attacker guesses a complete run of the sender that fits the puzzle and everything learned so far, and computes all of that run's hashes at once. Each round either opens the puzzle or uncovers at least one more hash the real sender used, and there are only N of those.

This attack is expensive. Each round makes a huge number of hash calls, and the version with N rounds also needs unlimited computation between rounds; an efficient version needs about N/ε rounds to succeed except with probability ε. So the result is a limit on what any proof can promise, not a cheap way to open puzzles. The chained puzzle needs about N rounds, so no hash-only puzzle can provably do better.

In practice: no proof can promise that a hash-only puzzle keeps an attacker busy for longer than the sender's total number of hashes, run one after another at the attacker's speed. So a one-minute lock against a 3.5 ns attacker costs the sender at least 234 hashes, spread over as many cores as it likes. The chained puzzle pays exactly this price.

Delay encryption is out of reach

Suppose a public setup costs S hash calls, after which senders can encrypt to slot 1234 with E hash calls, while computing the slot's key takes T seconds of sequential work. Then running the setup and encrypting a random message is itself a time-lock puzzle that costs S + E hash calls to build. By the result above, its message can be recovered in about S + E rounds. If the setup is cheap, the delay is limited by the cost of one encryption, which is the opposite of what delay encryption promises.

This argument does not settle the case of a long setup that itself uses the hash: then S + E is large and the bound says nothing useful. If, in addition, each slot has only one key that passes a quick public check, computing it is a verifiable delay function, and those are impossible from hashes alone even with an expensive private setup (Mahmoody, Smith and Wu 2020; Guan, Riazanov and Yuan 2025; Abusalah et al. 2026).

Batching and puzzle size

Independent puzzles share nothing, so solving one teaches nothing about another, and no method is known that opens several for less than a full sequential solve each. A sender can still put many transactions under one key and one puzzle. The version that would help a mempool, where unrelated senders encrypt privately to a common slot without knowing its key, is delay encryption, covered above.

On size: each chain the sender runs in parallel needs its own hidden link of about b bytes, so a chained puzzle that lets the sender work g ways in parallel has about g · b bytes. Whether some cleverer hash-only puzzle can be smaller is open. Lower bounds of this kind on communication are known only for restricted kinds of key exchange (Haitner et al. 2019), not for time-lock puzzles.

6. Implementation and measurements

chainseal implements section 3 as a complete, versioned format. A sender builds puzzles in advance and, when a transaction is ready, encrypts it under one. Anyone can then open the result in one of two ways: with the key, if the sender reveals it, or by solving the puzzle. A solve saves its progress every 10 seconds, so it can be stopped and resumed, and several puzzles can be solved at once, one per core. The code is about 4,000 lines of Rust, whose only dependencies are getrandom for the operating system's random numbers and signal-hook for stopping a solve cleanly. It has been run on macOS on Apple M4 and M4 Max laptops and on Linux on an AMD EPYC 9554P, and continuous integration builds and tests it on Linux for x86-64 and AArch64 and on macOS. A separate program of about 560 lines measures the chain step on Apple GPUs with Metal.

Encryption uses only SHA-256. The key gives an HMAC-SHA256 keystream, and a 32-byte HMAC tag covers the whole sealed transaction, so whoever lacks the key cannot change the puzzle or the ciphertext without the change being detected when the transaction is opened. A sealed transaction of L bytes takes 128 + n·b + L bytes:

How it was checked

  • A written specification fixes every byte of every format, with test vectors: 7 valid cases and 51 malformed inputs that an implementation must reject.
  • A second implementation, in Python, shares no code with the Rust one; it uses Python's own SHA-256 and HMAC and its own parsers. The two agree on every test vector, and each opens the other's sealed messages.
  • Tests. Known answers for SHA-256 and HMAC, every SHA-256 backend against plain SHA-256, round trips over many parameter sets, and end-to-end tests of the command-line tool, including interrupting and resuming a solve. A property test feeds every decoder 480,000 mutated encodings, and cargo-fuzz ran each decoder for two minutes under AddressSanitizer, about 108 million inputs in all; neither found a fault. The only unsafe code is in the two hardware backends.
  • A security audit of the code and the specification by an AI model, GPT-6 Astra. It ran in nine rounds, each checking the fixes for the previous one. The first four found 10 blockers, 3 major findings, 12 minor ones and a nit, and the fifth found nothing new. Rounds 6 to 9 checked the derivation of the key from every chain end. They found that the raw mask leaked the previous end and a bug in resuming a finished solve, both fixed. They also built attackers against 1- and 2-byte links that defeat two sharper versions of the guessing bound (Appendix A); the main text uses only the proved theorem. The ninth round found no blocker or major finding. The reports and the fixes are in the repository. The audit covered an earlier C implementation; the Rust one that replaced it reproduces its test vectors and command-line behaviour and was reviewed against it.

Limits of this evaluation. No human cryptographer has reviewed the code or the format. The x86 version, which uses the SHA-NI instructions, was built, tested and timed on one x86 machine, a 4-core AMD EPYC 9554P cloud VM. All other timings are from two Apple laptops. The GPU figures come from a separate benchmark program, not from the chainseal tool, which does not yet build puzzles on a GPU.

Where the building time comes from

A sender spends N / Rs = T / (ℓ · Rs) seconds building a T-second lock, so the cost is best stated as a ratio: 1/(ℓ · Rs) seconds of building per second of delay. It is the product of two factors. The assumed attacker computes one chained hash 6.6 times faster than one of the laptop's cores (3.5 ns against 23.2 ns), and the laptop as a whole hashes 10.6 times faster than one core running one chain (461 M/s against 43.3 M/s); 6.6 / 10.6 ≈ 0.62. The measured ratio, 0.70, is a little higher, because building one puzzle does not keep every core at its best rate to the end. The number of hashes cannot be reduced (section 5), so only these two factors can change: the attacker's assumed advantage, and the sender's total hash rate. Neither needs to change much for the scheme to be usable, because the building time is not spent while the transaction waits.

  • Build in advance. A puzzle does not depend on the transaction it will seal, so a wallet can keep a stock of puzzles and build more while the machine is idle or charging. The building time is then a cost per transaction, not a delay. On the laptop's CPU, all cores running for a day would build about 2,100 one-minute puzzles; most users send far fewer transactions.
  • Build in bulk on a GPU. A GPU has no SHA-256 instructions, but it has many more lanes. On the laptop, Metal computes 0.71 billion chained hashes per second, 1.5 times the CPU, which brings the ratio to 0.4 and the cost of a one-minute puzzle to 24 s. On the M4 Max, the GPU computes 2.96 billion per second, 3.4 times its CPU, or 5.8 s per puzzle. A lone chain on the GPU takes 5.1 µs per step, more than 200 times slower than one CPU core, so the GPU reaches these rates only with about 65,536 chains in flight, that is, thousands of 16-chain puzzles built at once. It suits building a stock, not one puzzle on demand. For the same reason, a GPU does not help an attacker, who needs one fast chain.
  • Use more hardware. The M4 Max laptop, with 16 cores, built a reference puzzle on its CPU in 30.1 s. A sender without capable hardware can outsource building to several services and combine their keys (section 5).
  • Lock for less time, or assume a slower attacker. Building time is proportional to the delay, and a transaction only needs to stay sealed until its place in the order can no longer change; if that happens when its block is proposed, one 12-second slot suffices. Halving the delay halves the total work N, the building time and the honest opening time, leaves the puzzle size unchanged and barely changes the bound of the theorem: against the same 3.5 ns attacker, a 30-second lock takes about 21 s to build on the laptop's CPU and a 15-second lock about 11 s. The number of chains does not affect security: the theorem depends on n only through P ≈ N. It sets the size, 24 bytes per chain, and how many cores can work on one puzzle at once. 8. Using it in Ethereum

  • Commit–reveal with a fallback. The user sends the encrypted transaction, the puzzle and the commitment C. After ordering, the user reveals K, and nodes check H(K) = C. The ordering must commit to the whole sealed transaction, not just to C: once K is known, anyone can seal a different transaction under the same puzzle. Solving is needed only for users who stay silent, and a deposit can charge them for it. A deposit that is forfeited when a solve fails also deters cancelling with a malformed puzzle. Execution rules must say what a transaction opened late may still do.
  • Opening silent users' puzzles needs dedicated hardware. To open within about T, solvers need chips as fast as the attacker the delay is priced against. On a laptop, a one-minute puzzle takes about 6.7 minutes. Either the network runs solver chips, or late-opened transactions execute in a later slot.
  • Solving cost. If every transaction in a 12-second slot, about 270 in the blocks we sampled, had to be opened by solvers, with one-minute puzzles, that would keep about 1,350 attacker-speed chain circuits busy at all times, or about 8,900 M4 cores. In the normal case almost none are needed, since users reveal their keys.
  • Bandwidth. Sealing adds 128 + n · b bytes to a transaction, 512 bytes with the reference parameters: the 452-byte puzzle and 60 bytes for the encryption. A signed ETH transfer is about 110 bytes, so sealed it takes 622 bytes, about 5.7 times more. Most transactions are larger than a transfer: in 20 consecutive mainnet blocks sampled on 22 October 2026, the median transaction was 183 bytes and the mean about 560, so sealing makes the median transaction about 3.8 times larger and the mean about twice as large.
  • Gas and block space. Sent as calldata, the added bytes are nearly all random and so pay the price of nonzero bytes: 16 gas each, or 40 under the floor of EIP-7623 that applies to transactions carrying mostly data. That is about 8,200–20,500 gas, against 21,000 for a plain transfer. At the base fee of about 0.34 gwei in the sample and an ETH price of $2,400, that is under 2 cents. EIP-7976, proposed for the Glamsterdam upgrade, would raise the floor to 64 gas per byte, about 33,000 gas. A contract should not store the puzzle: storage costs over 20,000 gas per 32-byte word, about 320,000 gas for 512 bytes. It needs to keep only the 32-byte hash of the sealed transaction, which binds the ordering to it, and the puzzle only has to stay available until it is opened. The sampled blocks held about 271 transactions and used 27 million gas each, so sealing every transaction would add about 2.2 million gas per block, a twelfth of current use. A blob carries about 127 KB at a separate and usually much lower price, enough for about 250 puzzles, and is kept for about 18 days, far longer than any puzzle needs.
  • Energy and phones. One puzzle took 42.1 s on the laptop's CPU at an estimated 20 W, about 0.23 Wh. That would be about 1% of a 20 Wh phone battery if a phone were as efficient; no phone was measured. Current phones have 6 to 8 CPU cores, most of them efficiency cores, and all have SHA-256 instructions. A wallet can build its stock of puzzles while charging (section 7), or outsource building to several services (section 5).
  • Spam. The bare construction gives no way to check, before solving, that a puzzle leads to its committed key, so a malformed puzzle is found only after a full solve. A proof system or a trusted builder could check it, at extra cost. Fees and deposits must be collectable without the key, and the number of open puzzles per sender should be capped.
  • Related designs. Threshold-encrypted mempools release keys from a committee: Shutter's keypers, Ferveo (Bebel and Ojha 2022) and FairBlock (Momeni, Gorbunov and Zhang 2022), with batched schemes that keep unselected transactions private (Choudhuri et al. 2024); Passerat-Palmbach (2026) surveys them. Radius's PVDE (2022) seals each transaction under an RSA time-lock puzzle with a zero-knowledge proof that the puzzle is well formed, and multiparty delay encryption (Khajehpour et al. 2023) opens a whole batch with one timed computation. Buterin proposed time-locked transactions as an alternative to commit–reveal in 2018, and later contrasted costly per-transaction time locks with delay encryption (2023, 2024). Voluntary opening backed by forced opening is the pattern of timed commitments (Boneh and Naor 2000). chainseal plays the role of PVDE's puzzle with a hash in place of RSA: it needs no setup and no committee, at the price of more work for the sender and a puzzle that cannot be checked in advance.

9. Open questions

For per-transaction time locks, no new construction is needed: the chained puzzle is optimal up to a constant factor among random-oracle constructions, its delay is proved in the random-oracle model (though the proof has not been checked by machine or by independent reviewers), the parameters are workable, and an implementation exists. What is missing is a better estimate of the attacker's hardware, measurements on more hardware, and the network design. For delay encryption, new ideas are needed, and they must go beyond hashes, at least when the setup is cheap. The open work, roughly in order of importance:

  1. How fast can chained SHA-256 be? Every parameter is priced against this. A careful circuit-level estimate, ideally from chip designers, would turn the advantage factor A ≈ 3.7, that is 3.5 ns, into a defensible number. An alternative is a hash whose sequential speed differs less between laptops and custom chips.
  2. A sharper bound for short links. A reviewed proof of the bound in Appendix A would allow 16-byte links and save 128 bytes per puzzle.
  3. Measurements on more hardware, and building on GPUs. chainseal has been timed on two Apple laptops and one x86 server core (37 ns per chained hash), and the chain step on two Apple GPUs. Timing it on x86 desktops, phones and discrete GPUs would replace the remaining estimates in section 7, and the tool should build batches of puzzles on a GPU itself.
  4. A lower bound on puzzle size. Chained puzzles need about b bytes per chain the sender runs in parallel. Does every hash-only time lock that lets the sender work g ways in parallel need size proportional to g, or is there a smaller puzzle?
  5. Mechanism design. Who runs solvers and how they are paid (a published key can be copied, so the reward cannot simply go to whoever posts it first), who pays for silent users, how late-opened transactions execute, how malformed puzzles are handled, and how the puzzle bytes fit the bandwidth budget.
  6. Delay encryption beyond hashes. Compare isogeny, lattice and batch-solvable constructions on setup, quantum security and cost against the hash-only baseline measured here.

Appendix A. A sharper bound for short links

Nothing in the main text depends on this appendix. It records what we know about short links, in case a smaller puzzle is worth the extra assumption.

The theorem of section 4 charges the attacker as if one right guess of a link could save all the remaining work, so its guessing term, Q / 28b, does not depend on how much is saved. That is probably loose. Because the key needs the end of every chain, a guessed link is confirmed only when the attacker's own chains reach it, and until then every run from a guess is blind. So a guess that saves a whole chain has itself cost a whole chain of hashes. This suggests:

Conjecture (random-oracle model)

An attacker that makes at most Q queries saves at least d rounds against the honest opener with probability at most about 2Q / (28b · min(d, m)), plus terms of order QP / 2256.

For 16-byte links, a one-minute lock with n = 16 and R = 1021 H/s, this is about 2−81 for saving a whole chain or more. It does not help small savings: for saving one round it is about 2−51, no better than the theorem. The cap at one chain cannot be removed. The security audit (section 6) built attackers against 1- and 2-byte links that defeat two stronger versions: charging each guess its whole saving fails against an attacker that merges guessed runs reaching the same link, and squaring the probability for savings of more than half fails against one that prepares short starts for every value of a link.

Status. An AI model, GPT-6 Astra, wrote a proof of the conjecture with the constant 2, in the model of the theorem without preprocessing. A second model, Claude Opus, checked it step by step and against the known attacks, and found gaps in rigor that it judged fixable but no error. A separate search for counterexamples at toy sizes found none. No person has reviewed the proof, so we do not rely on it. If it holds, 16-byte links would make the puzzle 324 bytes instead of 580, while savings of less than a chain would still be bounded only by the theorem.
